Bathroom Partition Mounting Styles

Every bathroom partition is held up one of four ways: from the floor with a headrail across the top, from the floor alone, from steel above the ceiling, or from both the floor and the ceiling.

These four bathroom partition mounting styles decide what your building has to provide, how the stalls clean, and how well they stand up to abuse. Pick the style first, then the material.

The four mounting styles

Overhead braced

Pilasters (the posts at the front of each stall) are fastened to the floor, and an anodized aluminum anti-grip headrail runs across their tops and ties into the walls. ASI calls it "economical and sturdy" and says it "installs just about anywhere." Overhead braced partitions →

Floor mounted (floor anchored)

Pilasters are bolted into the floor with no headrail. The anchors do all the work, so manufacturers call for a structural concrete floor: Bobrick specifies anchors with at least 2" of penetration into structural concrete at least 3" thick. With nothing above the doors, the restroom looks more open. Floor mounted partitions →

Ceiling hung

Pilasters hang from structural steel above the finished ceiling and never touch the floor. Paired with wall-hung toilets, the whole floor is open for cleaning. The building has to supply that steel, and it has to be engineered for the load. Ceiling hung partitions →

Floor to ceiling

Pilasters are anchored into the concrete floor and into structural support at the ceiling. ASI describes the style as "extremely stable and durable," and Hadrian recommends it for high-traffic areas such as schools. Floor to ceiling partitions →

How to choose

Start with the building.

  • No structure above the ceiling, or you don't want ceiling work? Overhead braced or floor mounted. Bobrick recommends these two when "adequate ceiling support cannot be provided."
  • Floor can't take anchors? Ceiling hung is the only style with nothing in the floor. It needs engineered steel overhead.
  • High ceiling or open look? Floor mounted has no headrail. Hadrian says the "absence of top structure makes them the ideal choice for high ceiling areas."
  • Heavy use or abuse? Floor to ceiling anchors every pilaster at both ends. Pair it with a tough material such as phenolic, solid plastic (HDPE) or stainless steel.

Then check the material. Several manufacturers don't recommend ceiling-hung or floor-anchored mounting for solid plastic, according to the New York State OGS master specification. Scranton Products offers its Hiny Hiders HDPE partitions in floor mounted overhead-braced, ceiling hung or floor to ceiling configurations.

Heights and floor clearance

The mounting style changes how tall the pilasters are, not how tall the doors and panels are. Standard doors and panels are 58" high, mounted 12" above the finished floor, in Hadrian's standard series and on our stall dimensions guide. Solid plastic often runs 55" mounted 14" above the floor. Taller, lower-gap privacy versions are available in some lines.

If you lower the gap for privacy, check toe clearance in the wheelchair accessible stall. Under 2010 ADA Standards section 604.8.1.4, the front partition and at least one side partition need 9" of toe clearance above the finished floor (12" in children's compartments), with exceptions for larger compartments. See our ADA compliance guide.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most common toilet partition mounting style?

Overhead braced, meaning floor-anchored pilasters tied together by a headrail, is the general-purpose style. ASI calls it "economical and sturdy" and says it "installs just about anywhere." It needs no ceiling structure and works on floors that accept standard anchors, so it suits most restrooms.

Are there wall mounted toilet partitions?

Not for full stalls. Every style attaches panels and pilasters to the walls with brackets, but stalls still need support from the floor, the ceiling or both. "Wall-hung" mounting is offered for urinal screens only, for example in Bobrick's DuraLine compact laminate line.

Which mounting style is easiest to clean, and which is strongest?

Ceiling hung is easiest to clean, because nothing touches the floor (ASI, Hadrian). Floor to ceiling is the most stable, with every pilaster anchored at both ends (ASI: "extremely stable and durable").

Can I switch mounting styles on an existing restroom?

Sometimes. Ceiling hung and floor to ceiling need structural members at the ceiling, which aren't supplied with the partitions. Floor mounted needs structural concrete. Have your contractor or engineer confirm the structure first.
