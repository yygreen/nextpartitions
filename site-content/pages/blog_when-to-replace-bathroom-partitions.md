# When (and How) to Upgrade Your Commercial Bathroom Partitions

Most commercial bathroom partitions don't fail all at once. A hinge wears, a door sags, rust shows at the base, a panel swells. The question is when to stop fixing parts and replace bathroom partitions as a whole system. This guide covers the signs, when a part swap is enough, how to replace stalls without moving the toilets, how to upgrade while you're at it, the accessibility check, and the steps from measuring to installation.

One thing first: Next Partitions supplies partitions and parts. We don't install them. You hire a qualified installer directly.

## The parts that wear

![Parts of a toilet partition, front view](/images/diagrams/partition-anatomy.svg)

1. Headrail
2. Pilaster
3. Door
4. Hinges
5. Latch and keeper
6. Pilaster shoe

Hinges, latches, keepers, brackets and shoes are hardware. You can usually replace them one by one. Doors, panels and pilasters are the system itself. When those go, a new system is often the better spend.

## Signs it's time to act

- **Rust.** Painted steel can rust where the coating is damaged. Stainless resists corrosion, but tensioned stainless rods and bolts have cracked in the humid air of unwashed indoor pools.
- **Swelling or delamination.** Plastic laminate has a particleboard core that may swell with excessive moisture. Hosing it down can void its warranty. Swollen edges and lifting faces don't repair well.
- **Broken or sagging doors.** A door that sags or won't latch often needs a hinge adjustment, not a new door. A door that is dented, warped or delaminating can be replaced on its own.
- **Hardware failing.** Worn hinge cams, missing nylon pintles, loose keepers and broken latches. The pilaster shoe is often the first part to look worn, because mop water and cleaning chemicals sit against it.
- **Graffiti and scratches.** Graffiti is harder to deal with on painted metal. A deep scratch exposes the black core on black core phenolic and the dark kraft paper under laminate. Xylene-based markers can "ghost" into solid plastic.
- **An outdated look, or privacy complaints.** One manufacturer calls gaps and sightlines "among the most common complaints about today's toilet partitions".
- **Accessibility problems.** An accessible stall that's too small, a door with less than 32" of clear opening, or hardware that needs tight grasping or twisting.
- **A remodel.** If the layout, fixtures or finishes are changing, the stalls usually change with them.

## Repair or replace?

Repair makes sense when the panels, doors and pilasters are sound. Replace when they aren't, or when many parts fail at once.

| Problem | A part swap is usually enough | Consider replacing the system |
|---|---|---|
| Door sags or won't latch | Adjust the bottom hinge cam, re-align the keeper, replace a worn cam ([hinges](/hardware/hinges), [latches](/hardware/locks-latches)) | Pilasters are loose or out of plumb across the run |
| One or two damaged doors | A [replacement door](/hardware/doors) on the existing pilasters | Several doors, panels and pilasters are damaged |
| Worn brackets, shoes or fasteners | New [structural parts](/hardware/parts) matched to your system | Many parts failing at once |
| Rust or swelling | Rarely: these affect the panel itself | Usually, and in a wet room choose a different material |
| Gaps and privacy | Privacy strips or continuous hinges, where your line offers them | No-sightline doors or taller doors, which need matching pilasters |
| Layout or fixtures changing | Not a repair job | Yes |

Two limits on repairs:

- **Hardware isn't universal.** Hole patterns, hinge styles and fasteners differ by brand and material. Latches aren't interchangeable across brands by default. Send the brand, material, thickness and photos with any [hardware request](/hardware).
- **Color matching has limits.** Colors change over the years and old doors fade, so a new door may not match exactly. Ask for a real sample if the match matters.

## How to replace stalls without changing the layout

Most replacements keep the toilets where they are. That means keeping the compartments where they are too.

1. **Measure what's there.** Record each compartment's width and depth, the pilaster positions from the side wall, the door widths, swing and hand, and the panel and pilaster thickness. Measure the door itself, not the opening. Our [stall dimensions guide](/guides/bathroom-stall-dimensions) shows what each figure means.
2. **Measure the room.** Wall length, room depth, ceiling height, toilet centerlines, and the floor and ceiling construction. See [how to measure a restroom](/guides/restroom-layout).
3. **Match the pilaster positions.** New pilasters in the old positions keep each toilet centered in its stall. In an accessible stall the toilet centerline must sit 16" to 18" from the side wall or partition (2010 ADA Standards 604.2).
4. **Keep the mounting style if the building suits it.** Pilaster heights differ by [mounting style](/mounting-styles), about 69" floor mounted and 82" to 83" overhead braced, so a style change means new pilasters made for that style.
5. **Reuse anchor locations where you can.** Every wall bracket needs solid backing behind it. Pilasters and brackets that land where the old ones were hit floor and backing that already carried a partition. New positions need backing confirmed before anything is drilled. Swapping short brackets for continuous ones can also mean new holes.

The installer then works from the approved shop drawings, not from the old stalls. If a wall isn't where the drawing says, resolve it before installing.

## How to upgrade while you replace

### A better material for the room
In showers, pools and locker rooms, look at [phenolic](/partitions/phenolic) or [solid plastic (HDPE)](/partitions/solid-plastic-hdpe). [Plastic laminate](/partitions/plastic-laminate) belongs in dry rooms. [Powder coated steel](/partitions/powder-coated-steel) suits dry rooms on a budget, and [stainless steel](/partitions/stainless-steel) suits premium public restrooms. Solid plastic must pass NFPA 286 under the IBC, so specify the fire-rated version.

Warranties are one way to compare. There is no standard term, and published warranties vary by brand and line:

| Material | Typical published warranty |
|---|---|
| Solid plastic (HDPE) | 15 to 25 years |
| Phenolic | 3 to 25 years; many lines 25 |
| Stainless steel | 5 to 15 years |
| Plastic laminate | 1 to 5 years |
| Powder coated steel | 1 to 4 years |

Most exclude vandalism, misuse and improper care. Read the full warranty for the line you choose. Compare all five on the [partitions page](/partitions).

### More privacy
Replacement is the easiest time to close the gaps. Options include no-sightline doors that overlap the pilaster, continuous brackets at the panel joints, and taller doors mounted closer to the floor. Taller doors don't work with every mounting style; some lines offer their tallest doors only overhead braced or floor to ceiling. The details are in [why do bathroom stalls have gaps](/blog/why-do-bathroom-stalls-have-gaps).

### A different mounting style
Switching styles depends on the building:

- **Ceiling hung** needs structural steel above the ceiling at every pilaster. Wood framing won't do.
- **Floor to ceiling** needs structural members at the ceiling and a concrete floor.
- **Floor mounted** needs structural concrete of adequate thickness at every pilaster.
- **Overhead braced** asks the least of the building.

Solid plastic is generally not offered floor mounted. Have your contractor or engineer confirm the structure first. Compare the styles in [ceiling hung vs floor mounted vs floor to ceiling](/blog/ceiling-hung-vs-floor-mounted-vs-floor-to-ceiling).

## Check accessibility when you replace

New stalls in an existing restroom can count as an alteration under the ADA. The Justice Department's rule for businesses lists remodeling, renovation and changes to the layout of full-height partitions as alterations. Normal maintenance isn't one, unless it affects usability.

When elements are altered, each altered element must meet the 2010 ADA Standards (202.3). An alteration may not leave a building less accessible than the new construction rules require (202.3.1). Where full compliance is technically infeasible, the work must comply to the maximum extent feasible. Where there are toilet compartments, at least one must be wheelchair accessible, and six or more compartments (or toilets and urinals combined) also need an ambulatory one (213.3.1).

In practice, check the accessible stall's size, door, hardware and toe clearance against our [ADA compliance guide](/guides/ada-compliance) before you request new stalls. Replacing the stalls may be the moment the old layout has to change.

## Step by step: from old stalls to new

1. **Measure.** Existing stalls and the room, as above. Take photos of the whole run, the floor, the ceiling and the hardware, with a tape measure in the shot. Note the brand if you can find it.
2. **Choose the material and mounting style.** Start from the room: wet or dry, the traffic it gets, and what the floor and ceiling can carry.
3. **Get a quote.** Send the measurements, photos and preferences. The quote names the brand and its warranty and includes the hardware. Approve the shop drawings before anything is made.
4. **Schedule installation with an installer.** You hire, schedule and pay the installer directly. Ask whether the price includes removing and disposing of the old stalls and filling old holes. The work should follow the manufacturer's instructions and your approved drawings. Our [installation guide](/guides/how-to-install-toilet-partitions) explains what happens on site.
5. **Plan the downtime.** Ask the installer how long the restroom will be closed. If you have more than one restroom, replace one at a time. Panels should be stored flat when they arrive, not leaned against a wall. Solid plastic may need about 24 hours at room temperature before and after installation, so check your manufacturer's instructions.

## FAQ

### When should you replace toilet partitions instead of repairing them?
When doors, panels or pilasters are damaged, rusted or swollen, when many parts fail at once, or when the layout is changing. If the panels and pilasters are sound, replacing the hardware or a door is usually enough.

### Can I replace just one stall door?
Usually. A replacement door that matches the brand, size and hardware can hang on the existing pilaster. Old colors may have faded, so ask for a sample.

### Can new partitions use the existing layout?
Yes, if the existing compartments still meet the plumbing code and accessibility rules. Match the pilaster positions so the toilets stay centered, and check the accessible stall before you request new partitions.

### Do I need to bring my restroom up to ADA standards when I replace the stalls?
It depends on your project. Under the 2010 ADA Standards, altered elements must comply (202.3), and an alteration can't reduce accessibility. Ask your architect or code official whether your replacement counts as an alteration.

### Can I change the mounting style when I replace?
Sometimes. Ceiling hung needs steel above the ceiling, floor mounted needs structural concrete, and floor to ceiling needs both a concrete floor and ceiling structure. Overhead braced works in the most buildings.
