# Why Do Bathroom Stalls Have Gaps? (And How to Eliminate Them)

Why do bathroom stalls have gaps? Mostly because a traditional US partition is a modular kit: factory-made doors and panels assembled on site between real walls, with room left for the door to swing, for the floor to be cleaned and for the walls to be out of square. Those bathroom stall gaps are also the most visible privacy complaint about restrooms. Bobrick calls gaps and sightlines "among the most common complaints about today's toilet partitions", and Bradley's partitions brochure says almost half of Americans are uncomfortable or bothered by the small gap between doors and pilasters.

The good news: Bobrick, ASI, Bradley and Scranton Products all sell partitions that close most or all of the gaps. Here's where the gaps come from, how big they are, and how to get rid of them.

## The four gaps in a standard stall

![The four gaps in a standard bathroom stall, front view](/images/diagrams/stall-gaps.svg)

1. **The door edges.** The thin vertical gaps between the door and the pilaster on the hinge side and the latch side. These are the "sightlines" people complain about.
2. **Under the doors and panels.** On standard partitions the doors and panels are 58" tall and mounted 12" above the floor (Bobrick, ASI and Bradley all list this as standard).
3. **Above the doors and panels.** With 58" doors at 12", the top of the stall is at 70", so a tall person can see over.
4. **At the wall.** Where a panel or pilaster meets the building wall.

## Why the gaps exist

### The door needs room to swing
ASI explains that the gaps on the hinge and latch sides "exist to allow the door to swing freely", so it doesn't catch on the pilaster. On a traditional square-edged door, that clearance is a straight gap you can see through.

### Site tolerances
Partitions are built to measured sizes in a factory, then installed between walls that ASI notes "may not be perfectly plumb, level, or square". Gaps at the wall and at the door edges absorb the difference, which is why the modular system is forgiving to install.

### Cleaning and occupancy
According to ASI, the bottom gap "exists to simplify cleaning and to provide visible occupancy indication": a mop reaches under the stall, and you can see feet without knocking. ASI traces the open-bottom design to early 20th-century buildings, where maintenance access and airflow took priority over privacy.

### Cost
ASI also points out that the traditional system spread because it is "fast to manufacture, inexpensive to ship, and forgiving to install". If budget matters, ask us to quote standard and gap-free options side by side.

### Accessibility rules limit how low you can go
In a wheelchair-accessible stall, the 2010 ADA Standards (604.8.1.4) require 9" of toe clearance under the front partition and at least one side partition, 6" deep, so a wheelchair user's feet fit under the panel. Larger stalls are exempt: no front toe clearance is needed above 62" deep with a wall-hung toilet or 65" deep with a floor-mounted one, and no side toe clearance above 66" wide. That's why manufacturers' low-gap lines often keep the accessible stall at 9" (Bradley, for example, offers 72" doors at 6" above the floor, and 69" doors at 9" for ADA stalls).

## How big are the gaps?

Manufacturers rarely publish door-gap sizes. Bobrick does, for its traditional partitions:

| Where | Bobrick traditional partitions |
|---|---|
| Door to pilaster, hinge side | 3/32" |
| Door to pilaster, keeper (latch) side | 7/64" maximum |
| Panel or pilaster to wall | 1/2" to 1" |
| Under doors and panels (standard height) | 12" |

Bobrick describes these as "below industry standard", so other partitions may have wider door gaps. Floor clearance varies by line: ASI's solid plastic No Sight-55 line uses 55" doors and panels at 14" above the floor.

The installer sets most of these gaps when the parts go in. The drawing shows the gaps in orange on a door and on a panel, both resting on a 12" spacer while they are fixed.

![Gaps set at installation: door to pilaster, pilaster to wall, panel to back wall and panel to pilaster](/images/diagrams/install-gaps.svg)

1. **Wall pilaster to wall:** 1/2" to 1-1/4"
2. **Door to pilaster, hinge side:** 1/8"
3. **Door to pilaster, latch side:** 1/8"
4. **Spacer under door or panel:** 12"
5. **Panel to pilaster:** 1/2"
6. **Panel to back wall:** 1"

## How to reduce or eliminate the gaps

### 1. Overlapping door edges (no-sightline doors)
The most effective fix for the door-edge gaps is a door that overlaps the pilaster instead of butting against it, so there's no straight line to see through. Each manufacturer has its own version:

- **Bradley No-Site:** a rabbeted edge on the hinge and latch sides of the door. Bradley makes it standard on all its phenolic partitions.
- **ASI Zero-Sightline:** overlapping doors and pilasters on ASI's phenolic Ultimate Privacy partitions.
- **Scranton Products shiplap edges:** a shiplap cut edge on Hiny Hiders and Eclipse HDPE partitions removes the sightlines without extra hardware.
- **Bobrick Gap-Free Doors & Stiles:** an interlocking, sightline-free design, offered on DuraLineSeries (compact laminate) and SierraSeries partitions.

All four of these are on solid materials: [phenolic](/partitions/phenolic), [solid plastic (HDPE)](/partitions/solid-plastic-hdpe) and Bobrick's compact laminate and SCRC.

### 2. Privacy strips and continuous hinges
Steel partitions close the gaps with hardware instead:

- **ASI Integrated Privacy System:** integrated hinge-side and latch-side privacy components plus continuous channels, standard on ASI's [stainless steel](/partitions/stainless-steel) and [powder coated steel](/partitions/powder-coated-steel) partitions.
- **Bradley:** full-height No-Site strips and full-height continuous hinges and brackets for its stainless steel partitions.

If you're replacing worn doors or hardware, ask in your quote request whether a privacy strip or continuous hinge is available for your existing line. See also [replacement parts](/hardware/parts).

### 3. Taller doors, lower to the floor
Raising the top and lowering the bottom shrinks the gaps under and over the stall:

| Manufacturer | Taller options |
|---|---|
| Bobrick | 71 3/4" doors and panels at 4 1/2" floor clearance (DuraLineSeries, SierraSeries, DesignerSeries); 73" to 96" at 1" (DuraLineSeries, overhead braced and floor to ceiling only) |
| ASI | Ultimate Privacy 64 and 72: 64" or 72" doors and panels at 6" or 9" above the floor |
| Bradley | 72" at 6" above the floor; 69" at 9" for ADA stalls |
| Scranton Products | Eclipse in 55", 62" and 72" heights |

Taller doors don't work with every mounting style. ASI's 72" models list only floor-mounted overhead braced and floor-to-ceiling mounting, and Bradley offers its extra heights in the same two styles. Compare [overhead braced](/mounting-styles/overhead-braced) and [floor to ceiling](/mounting-styles/floor-to-ceiling) mounting.

### 4. Full-height stalls
For no gaps at all, look at full-height enclosures. Scranton's Aria partitions go from floor to ceiling, and Scranton says there's "no open space above or below", with a door that closes without a gap. Bobrick's PRIVADA cubicles come with 72" doors and panels at 9" floor clearance as standard, and extended heights up to 96" at 4".

Note that [floor-to-ceiling mounting](/mounting-styles/floor-to-ceiling) on its own describes the pilasters, which run from floor to ceiling; standard doors and panels on that mounting still have gaps above and below. Ask for full-height or extended-height doors and panels if that's what you want.

## Before you specify a gap-free restroom

- **Check the accessible stalls.** Low panels need the 9" toe clearance in wheelchair-accessible compartments unless the compartment is oversized (604.8.1.4). See the [ADA compliance guide](/guides/ada-compliance).
- **Check the ceiling.** Extended heights and full-height stalls need enough ceiling height, and floor-to-ceiling mounting needs a ceiling structure that can take the anchors.
- **Ask your engineer about air, light and sprinklers.** Closing a stall off changes how air, light and sprinkler coverage reach it.
- **Plan for occupancy.** Without a gap under the door you can't see whether a stall is in use, so add occupancy indicator latches. ASI includes them in its Integrated Privacy System, and Bradley's phenolic doors come pre-drilled for indicator latches.
- **Measure carefully.** Use our [stall dimensions guide](/guides/bathroom-stall-dimensions), and send photos of the walls and floor with your quote request.

## FAQ

### Why do bathroom stalls have gaps?
Traditional partitions leave gaps so the door can swing without catching, so installers can fit factory-made parts between walls that aren't perfectly square, and so floors can be cleaned and occupied stalls spotted. The modular design is also quick to make, ship and install.

### How big is the gap under a bathroom stall door?
On standard partitions, 12": the doors and panels are 58" tall and mounted 12" above the floor. Some solid plastic lines use 55" doors at 14".

### Can you get bathroom stalls without gaps?
Yes. Bradley, ASI, Scranton Products and Bobrick all sell no-sightline doors that overlap the pilaster, and taller doors mounted closer to the floor. Full-height stalls such as Scranton's Aria close the top and bottom too.

### Can I fix the gaps in my existing stalls?
Sometimes. Some manufacturers sell privacy strips and continuous hinges (Bradley offers both for its stainless steel partitions). Whether they fit your stalls depends on the brand and material, so send photos and the brand with your quote request.

### Do no-gap stalls meet ADA requirements?
They can, but in wheelchair-accessible stalls the front partition and one side partition need 9" of toe clearance unless the stall is oversized (2010 ADA Standards 604.8.1.4). Confirm with your architect or code official.
