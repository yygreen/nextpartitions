## The right material for the Washington, DC climate

Washington, DC gets about 42 inches of rain a year, on roughly 114 days, and relative humidity averages 74% in the morning and 53% in the afternoon. In most Washington, DC restrooms the room decides the material, not the weather:

- **Air-conditioned restrooms** (offices, restaurants, clinics, most schools): all five materials work. Choose on looks, cleaning routine and budget.
- **Restrooms without air conditioning, or that get wet from the inside** (warehouses, plants, parks, stadiums, pool and locker rooms): choose [solid plastic (HDPE)](/partitions/solid-plastic-hdpe) or [phenolic](/partitions/phenolic). Both are solid all the way through and handle constant moisture.

[Plastic laminate](/partitions/plastic-laminate) has a particleboard core that may swell with excessive moisture, and [powder coated steel](/partitions/powder-coated-steel) can rust where the coating is damaged, so keep both for dry rooms. [Stainless steel](/partitions/stainless-steel) resists corrosion and suits premium public restrooms. Compare all five on the [partitions page](/partitions).

## The rules a Washington, DC restroom has to meet

- **Wheelchair access:** a restroom with stalls needs at least one stall a wheelchair user can enter and use, and a room with six or more stalls and urinals also needs a narrower stall with grab bars on both sides. Federal law and the District's own building rules set the sizes, and the DC Department of Buildings applies them when it reviews permit plans.
- **Fire safety:** the District's building rules require solid plastic (HDPE) partitions to pass a room-corner fire test. Ask for the fire-rated version.
- **How many stalls:** the District's building rules set the number of toilets and urinals by how many people use the building. The table below shows what that means.
- **District extras:** the District's rules add an 18-inch vertical grab bar beside the toilet in accessible stalls, which the federal rules don't require, and set the narrower stall at 36 inches wide. A restaurant, store or other public venue that needs six or more toilets in total must also have a one-person family restroom.
- **Single-user and all-gender restrooms:** in Washington, DC a one-person public restroom must have a gender-neutral sign, such as "Restroom". A building may provide one-person restrooms open to everyone in place of separate men's and women's rooms. Rooms with several stalls are signed for men or women, and the District's building rules don't describe a shared all-gender room with stalls, so ask the DC Department of Buildings before planning one.
- **Permits:** the DC Department of Buildings requires a permit for interior alterations. Its list of work that needs no permit covers tiling, carpeting, cabinets, countertops and similar finish work, and movable partitions no taller than 5 feet 9 inches. It doesn't name toilet partitions. Ask the Department of Buildings whether your replacement needs one. You don't need a permit to buy partitions.

Rules change, so confirm your project with the DC Department of Buildings or your architect. The accessible stall's sizes are in our [ADA compliance guide](/guides/ada-compliance), and fixture spacing is in the [restroom layout guide](/guides/restroom-layout).

## How many stalls a Washington, DC restroom needs

Washington, DC counts toilets by a building's capacity: the most people it is designed to hold at one time, worked out from its floor area. It isn't the number of staff or of visitors in a day. Half are counted as men and half as women. Every toilet needs a stall, and every urinal needs a screen on each side. Urinals are optional: in a men's room they may replace up to half of the toilets in an office and up to two thirds in a restaurant or school, so the table shows the men's side as stalls.

:::stalls
head: Office | Restaurant | School
50: 1/1 | 1/1 | 1/1
100: 2/2 | 1/1 | 1/1
200: 3/3 | 2/2 | 2/2
400: 5/5 | 3/3 | 4/4
:::

These are minimums for the whole building, and at least one stall in each room must be wheelchair accessible. A men's room that needs only one toilet can't swap it for a urinal. A space designed for 15 people or fewer may have one shared restroom, and one-person restrooms open to everyone may take the place of separate men's and women's rooms.

To find your building's capacity, the District's building rules count an office at one person per 100 square feet of floor, a restaurant dining room at one per 15 square feet and a classroom at one per 20.

## What installation labor and sales tax add in Washington, DC

- **Local labor rates:** carpenters in the Washington–Arlington–Alexandria area earn a median of $29.23 an hour, about the same as the national median of $29.12. It's a guide to local labor rates, not a quote: installers set their own prices.
- **Public jobs:** on District government construction contracts over $2,000, schools and city buildings included, federal law requires at least the wage the U.S. Department of Labor sets for Washington, DC. For a carpenter on building work that is $34.41 an hour plus $14.33 in benefits.
- **Sales tax:** 6% in Washington, DC. The District has one rate, with no county or city tax on top. The rate is set to rise to 7% on October 1, 2027.
- **Tax on installation:** in Washington, DC, installation labor is left out of the taxed price when it is listed separately on the invoice. Minor repair and maintenance work on a building is taxed as a service, but work done as part of construction or major repairs is not. A contractor who supplies materials that become part of the building pays the tax when buying them.
- **Buying without installation:** partitions shipped to Washington, DC are taxable. A freight charge listed separately on the invoice is left out of the taxed price when the shipping happens after the sale. If the seller doesn't collect the tax, the buyer owes it to the District as use tax.

How tax applies depends on your contract, so confirm with your accountant or the DC Office of Tax and Revenue.
