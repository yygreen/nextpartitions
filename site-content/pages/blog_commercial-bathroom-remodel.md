# Commercial Bathroom Remodel: Steps, Costs, and What to Know

A commercial bathroom remodel has more rules attached than any other room in the building. The plumbing code fixes how far apart the fixtures sit, the ADA decides the size of at least one stall, and the structure decides how the partitions can be mounted. Get those three settled on paper and the rest of the remodel, from tile to toilet partitions, falls into a clear order.

## Decide how far the remodel goes

| Scope | What changes | What it means for the stalls |
|---|---|---|
| Refresh | Finishes, hardware, worn doors | Replace parts or doors on the existing pilasters |
| Replace in place | New partitions, same layout | New stalls built to the existing toilet positions |
| Full remodel | Layout, fixtures or walls move | A new layout, new shop drawings and new accessibility checks |

The scope matters because of the next section: the more you change, the more of the current code applies.

## The ADA rules a remodel triggers

Remodeling, renovation and changes to the layout count as alterations under the ADA. Normal maintenance doesn't. Three rules follow:

- **What you alter must comply.** Each altered element has to meet the 2010 ADA Standards, and an alteration can't leave the room less accessible than before. Where full compliance is technically infeasible, the work must comply as far as it feasibly can.
- **Accessible stalls.** Where a restroom has toilet compartments, at least one must be wheelchair accessible. Six or more compartments, or six or more toilets and urinals combined, also need an ambulatory accessible compartment.
- **The path of travel.** If you remodel an area that holds a primary function of the building, such as a dining room or an office floor, the route to it and the restrooms serving it must be made accessible too, unless that cost is disproportionate. The rule sets disproportionate at more than 20% of the cost of the alteration to the primary function area.

The accessible compartment is where most layouts change:

![Plan of a wheelchair accessible toilet compartment at minimum size, with the minimum area, toilet centerline, grab bars, door clear width and door location](/images/diagrams/ada-wheelchair-stall.svg)

It must be at least 60" wide, and at least 56" deep with a wall-hung toilet or 59" with a floor-mounted one. Its door needs 32" of clear opening and can't swing into that minimum area. The details are in our [ADA compliance guide](/guides/ada-compliance).

## Lay out the room

The layout starts from the wall the stalls stand on and the position of each toilet:

![Plan of an empty restroom seen from above, with the stall wall run, room depth, entry door and toilet centerlines lettered](/images/diagrams/measure-new-restroom-plan.svg)

- **A.** Room width along the wall where the stalls go.
- **B.** Room depth, from the stall wall to the opposite wall.
- **D.** Entry door location and which way it swings.
- **E.** Toilet centerlines, measured from the side wall.

The International Plumbing Code sets the spacing: a toilet's centerline at least 15" from a side wall or partition, urinals at least 30" apart center to center, and at least 21" clear in front of each fixture. A standard stall is typically 36" wide. How many fixtures a building needs comes from your local plumbing code, so confirm the count with your architect or code official before you fix the layout. The method is in our [restroom layout guide](/guides/restroom-layout).

## Choose the partition material for the room

Five materials are sold for toilet partitions, and a manufacturer's published price index shows how far apart they sit:

| Material | Price index (painted steel = 1.0) | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| [Powder coated steel](/partitions/powder-coated-steel) | 1.0 | Dry rooms on a budget |
| [Plastic laminate](/partitions/plastic-laminate) | 1.1 | Dry rooms where the finish should match the interior |
| [Solid plastic (HDPE)](/partitions/solid-plastic-hdpe) | 1.9 | Wet rooms, schools, rooms that are hosed down |
| [Phenolic](/partitions/phenolic) | 2.4 | Wet rooms and heavy use; a hard, slim panel |
| [Stainless steel](/partitions/stainless-steel) | 2.7 | Premium public restrooms |

Choose from the room, not from the index. A cheaper material in the wrong room is the expensive choice: laminate swells where water reaches its core, and painted steel rusts where the coating is damaged. If you choose HDPE, the International Building Code requires it to pass the NFPA 286 fire test, so ask for the fire-rated version.

## Let the building choose the mounting style

![The four mounting styles side by side: overhead braced, floor mounted, ceiling hung and floor to ceiling](/images/diagrams/mounting-styles-compare.svg)

- **Overhead braced** asks the least of the building and works with any ceiling.
- **Floor mounted** needs structural concrete at every pilaster.
- **Ceiling hung** needs structural steel above the finished ceiling at every pilaster.
- **Floor to ceiling** needs a concrete floor and structure at the ceiling.

A remodel is the one moment when the structure is open. If you want ceiling hung stalls, the steel goes in before the ceiling does. More in our [mounting style comparison](/blog/ceiling-hung-vs-floor-mounted-vs-floor-to-ceiling).

## The order of work

1. **Design and code review.** Settle the layout, the accessible stalls and the fixture count.
2. **Request the partitions.** Send the layout for a quote and approve the shop drawings. Partitions are made to order, so this starts well before demolition.
3. **Demolition and rough-in.** Old stalls and finishes out; plumbing and wiring moved.
4. **Wall backing.** Every partition bracket and every grab bar needs solid backing behind the wall. It has to go in while the walls are open.
5. **Finishes.** Floor and wall tile, ceiling, paint. Partitions are set out from the finished surfaces.
6. **Partitions.** Installed from the approved shop drawings.
7. **Accessories.** Grab bars, dispensers, mirrors and hooks.

## What drives the cost of the stalls

- **Material.** The index above: stainless and phenolic cost more than twice as much as painted steel.
- **The number of stalls and screens,** and the accessible stalls, which are larger and carry more hardware.
- **Privacy upgrades.** Taller doors, doors that overlap the pilaster and continuous hinges.
- **Freight.** Partitions ship by freight carrier, crated and palletized.
- **Installation.** Priced by the installer, separately from the partitions.

## FAQ

### What does a commercial bathroom remodel involve?
A code review, a layout, new fixtures and finishes, and new partitions and accessories. The stalls are made to order from approved shop drawings, so they are requested early and installed after the finishes.

### Does a commercial bathroom remodel have to meet ADA requirements?
The elements you alter must meet the 2010 ADA Standards, and an alteration can't reduce accessibility. Remodeling an area with a primary function can also require an accessible route and restrooms, up to 20% of that alteration's cost.

### How many ADA stalls does a remodeled restroom need?
At least one wheelchair accessible compartment where there are toilet compartments, plus an ambulatory one where there are six or more compartments, or six or more toilets and urinals combined.

### What is the cheapest material for commercial bathroom stalls?
Powder coated steel, followed closely by plastic laminate. Both suit dry rooms only.

### When should partitions be ordered in a remodel?
Early. They are made to order from approved shop drawings, and wall backing for their brackets has to be in place before the walls are closed.

### How long does a commercial bathroom remodel take?
It depends on the scope and on lead times for fixtures and partitions. Ask each supplier for its current lead time before you set the schedule.
