## The right material for Nashville's climate

Nashville gets about 51 inches of rain a year, on roughly 121 days, and relative humidity averages 84% in the morning and 56% in the afternoon. On average the wettest month is May (about 5.0 inches) and the driest is October (about 3.4 inches). What that means for partitions depends on whether the restroom is air-conditioned:

- **Air-conditioned restrooms** (offices, restaurants, clinics, most schools): all five materials work. Choose on looks, cleaning routine and budget.
- **Restrooms open to outside air or without air conditioning** (warehouses, plants, parks, stadiums, pool and locker rooms): choose [solid plastic (HDPE)](/partitions/solid-plastic-hdpe) or [phenolic](/partitions/phenolic). Both are solid all the way through and handle constant moisture.

Keep [plastic laminate](/partitions/plastic-laminate) and [powder coated steel](/partitions/powder-coated-steel) for dry rooms; [stainless steel](/partitions/stainless-steel) resists corrosion. Compare all five on the [partitions page](/partitions).

## The rules a Nashville restroom has to meet

- **Wheelchair access:** a restroom with stalls needs at least one stall a wheelchair user can enter and use. Tennessee law uses the federal access standards as its own for public buildings. Metro Nashville's building rules also apply a national accessibility standard, in its 2017 edition, and the plans are approved by the office that reviews the project.
- **Fire safety:** Nashville's building rules require solid plastic (HDPE) partitions to pass a room fire test. Ask for the fire-rated version.
- **How many stalls:** Nashville sets the number of toilets by how many people use the building. The table below shows what that means.
- **Tennessee and federal stall rules match:** Tennessee adopted the federal standards word for word, so a stall, door or grab bar that meets one meets the other. The 2017 standard in Nashville's building rules is a separate document, so ask Metro Codes and Building Safety which sizes it checks on your project.
- **Single-user and all-gender restrooms:** under Nashville's rules a one-person restroom is marked as open to everyone. A shared all-gender room is allowed if every toilet sits in its own compartment with a door and every urinal has privacy partitions. Since July 2026, state law requires shared restrooms in public colleges and universities, state prisons, juvenile detention facilities and domestic violence shelters to be designated for females, males or families. A 2021 law covers shared restrooms in public schools.
- **Permits:** Metro Nashville requires a commercial renovation permit for renovation or maintenance work in an existing business. Its list of that work includes doors and walls and doesn't name toilet partitions. Normal maintenance repairs, such as painting and floor finishing, may be made without a permit if the building official approves. Ask Metro Codes and Building Safety (615-862-6500) whether your replacement needs one. You don't need a permit to buy partitions.

Rules change, so confirm your project with Metro Codes and Building Safety or your architect. The accessible stall's sizes are in our [ADA compliance guide](/guides/ada-compliance), and fixture spacing is in the [restroom layout guide](/guides/restroom-layout).

## How many stalls a Nashville restroom needs

Nashville counts toilets by a building's capacity: the most people it is designed to hold at one time, worked out from its floor area. It isn't the number of staff or of visitors in a day. Half are counted as men and half as women, and every toilet needs a stall with a door. Urinals are optional: in a men's room they may replace up to half of the toilets in an office and up to two thirds in a restaurant or school, each with a privacy screen. The table shows toilets only.

:::stalls
head: Office | Restaurant | School
50: 1/1 | 1/1 | 1/1
100: 2/2 | 1/1 | 1/1
200: 3/3 | 2/2 | 2/2
400: 5/5 | 3/3 | 4/4
:::

These are minimums for the whole building, and at least one stall in each room must be wheelchair accessible. An office for 25 people or fewer, or any space for 15 or fewer, may use one shared room. Nashville adds one rule of its own: a restaurant in a mall, other than a food court tenant, must have its toilets inside its own space.

To find your building's capacity, the building rules count an office at one person per 150 square feet of floor, a restaurant dining room at one per 15 square feet and a classroom at one per 20. Your architect or Metro Codes and Building Safety sets the final number.

## What installation labor and sales tax add in Nashville

- **Local labor rates:** carpenters in the Nashville–Davidson–Murfreesboro–Franklin area earn a median of $26.81 an hour, about 8% below the national median of $29.12. It's a guide to local labor rates, not a quote: installers set their own prices.
- **Public jobs:** Tennessee's wage law for public work covers state highway projects only, so on a school or city building a set wage applies only where federal money brings in the federal wage rules. The federal rate for a carpenter on building work in Davidson County is $22.07 an hour, with no benefits amount listed.
- **Sales tax:** 9.75% in Nashville: 7% state, 2.25% Davidson County and a 0.5% transit surcharge. The 2.75% local part applies only to the first $1,600 of the price of each single item, and the state adds 2.75% on the part of an item's price between $1,600 and $3,200.
- **Tax on installation:** in Tennessee, labor to install something that becomes part of the building is not taxed when it is billed separately. A contractor who supplies and installs pays the tax on the materials and builds it into the price, rather than adding it to your invoice.
- **Buying without installation:** partitions shipped to Nashville are taxable, freight included when the seller bills it. If the seller doesn't collect the tax, the buyer owes it to the state.

How tax applies depends on your contract, so confirm with your accountant or the Tennessee Department of Revenue.
