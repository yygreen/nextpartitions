# Back to School: Is Your Restroom Ready?

A school restroom renovation rarely starts with a plan. It starts with a work order: a door that won't latch, a rusted panel, a stall students avoid. Summer is when a school can deal with all of them at once, and the weeks before it are when the decisions get made. Walk each restroom with this checklist while the building is still in session, so the damage is fresh and the list is complete.

## Inspect every stall, part by part

Each stall has the same six parts, and each one wears in its own way:

![Parts of a toilet partition, front view](/images/diagrams/partition-anatomy.svg)

1. Headrail
2. Pilaster
3. Door
4. Hinges
5. Latch and keeper
6. Pilaster shoe

Check each restroom for:

- **Doors that sag or won't latch.** Push each door closed and try the latch.
- **Loose pilasters.** Grip each one and rock it. A pilaster that moves is loose at the floor or the headrail.
- **Rust.** Look at the bottom edges of steel panels and at the pilaster shoes, where mop water sits.
- **Swollen or lifting edges** on plastic laminate, a sign that water has reached the core.
- **Missing or damaged hardware:** hinges, latches, keepers, coat hooks and fasteners.
- **Graffiti and scratches** that no longer clean off.
- **Gaps students complain about** at the door edges.

Take a photo of each problem with the room and stall number in the shot. The photos become the repair list, and they go with the quote request if stalls need replacing.

## Repair what can be repaired

Hinges, latches, keepers, brackets and shoes are hardware. They can usually be replaced one by one, as long as the new part matches the brand and material of the old one.

A door that won't latch is the most common repair, and it rarely needs a new door:

![Three causes of a door that won't latch: pilaster out of plumb, door dropped at the latch side, keeper out of line](/images/diagrams/door-latch-problems.svg)

- **A** Pilaster out of plumb: the gap at the latch edge is wider at the top than at the bottom. Re-set the pilaster.
- **B** Door dropped at the latch side: the latch sits below the keeper. Check that the hinges are plumb and their screws tight.
- **C** Keeper out of line with the latch: move the keeper so the latch slides freely into it.

A single damaged door can also be replaced on its existing pilasters. Colors fade over the years, so ask for a sample if the match matters. Parts and doors are on the [hardware pages](/hardware).

## Replace when the panels themselves fail

Repairs stop making sense when the doors, panels or pilasters are the problem:

| What you find | What it means |
|---|---|
| Rust through a steel panel | The panel is finished; in a wet room, choose a different material |
| Swollen or delaminating laminate | The core has taken on water and won't recover |
| Several doors, panels and pilasters damaged | A new system usually costs less than repairing each one |
| Pilasters loose across the whole run | The anchoring has failed, not one part |
| The layout or the fixtures are changing | The stalls change with them |

## Two checks before you replace

**Accessibility.** New stalls in an existing restroom can count as an alteration under the ADA, and altered elements must meet the 2010 ADA Standards. Check the accessible stall's size, door and toe clearance before you request new stalls, because replacing them may be the moment the old layout has to change. Stalls designed for children ages 3 to 12 have their own dimensions. The rules are in our [ADA compliance guide](/guides/ada-compliance).

**Fire rating.** If you're replacing with solid plastic (HDPE), the International Building Code requires it to pass the NFPA 286 fire test, and standard colors may not. Ask for the fire-rated version and its test report.

## Plan the summer backward from the first day

Partitions are made to order from approved shop drawings, so the work runs in a fixed sequence:

1. **Walk the restrooms and list the work,** building by building.
2. **Measure the rooms that need new stalls.** The six measurements are in [how to measure for toilet partitions](/blog/how-to-measure-for-toilet-partitions).
3. **Request one quote for all the restrooms,** with the photos and the grade levels each room serves.
4. **Approve the shop drawings.** Check them against each room first, because the partitions are made from them.
5. **Book the installer** for the weeks the building is empty, and ask whether the price includes removing the old stalls.
6. **Receive and store the delivery.** Store panels flat, not leaned against a wall. Solid plastic may need about 24 hours at room temperature before it's installed, so check the manufacturer's instructions.

Manufacturing and shipping sit between steps 4 and 6, and their length depends on the material and color. Ask for the current lead time with the quote, and count back from the first day of school.

## FAQ

### When should a school replace its bathroom stalls?
When doors, panels or pilasters are rusted, swollen or damaged, or when many parts fail at once. If the panels and pilasters are sound, replacing hardware or a door is usually enough.

### Can school bathroom stalls be repaired instead of replaced?
Often. Hinges, latches, keepers, brackets and shoes can be replaced one by one, and a single door can hang on its existing pilasters. The parts have to match the brand and material.

### What should a school restroom inspection cover?
Each stall's doors, latches, hinges, pilasters and shoes, plus rust, swollen edges, graffiti and the gaps at the door edges. Photograph each problem with the room and stall number.

### Does replacing the stalls trigger ADA requirements?
It can. New stalls in an existing restroom can count as an alteration, and altered elements must meet the 2010 ADA Standards. Ask your architect or code official.

### When is the best time to replace school bathroom stalls?
When the building is empty, usually over the summer. The partitions are made to order, so measure, quote and approve the drawings well before the break.
