# How to Design a Modern Commercial Bathroom

Commercial bathroom design is decided by the stalls more than by anything else in the room. They take up most of the floor, they set the sightlines from the door, and they are what a visitor touches. A modern restroom gets four things right: a layout that leaves room to move, privacy without gaps, finishes that suit how the room is cleaned, and an accessible stall that was designed in, not squeezed in.

## Start with the layout

The stalls stand along one wall, and the count comes from the length of that wall:

![Plan view of a row of toilet compartments, with the accessible, ambulatory and standard compartments](/images/diagrams/sheet-plan.svg)

- A standard stall is typically 36" wide. The plumbing code minimum is 30" wide and 60" deep with a floor-mounted toilet.
- The wheelchair accessible stall is at least 60" wide.
- Each fixture needs at least 21" of clear space in front of it.
- Doors, the sink and the entry all compete for the same floor, so draw the door swings before you fix the stall positions.

Place the entry so the open door doesn't show the stalls or the urinals from the corridor. The full method is in our [restroom layout guide](/guides/restroom-layout).

## Design the privacy in

Gaps are the most common complaint about restroom stalls. A standard stall has four of them:

![The four gaps in a standard bathroom stall, front view](/images/diagrams/stall-gaps.svg)

1. At the door edges
2. Under the doors and panels
3. Above the doors and panels
4. Where the partition meets the wall

Modern restrooms close them in three steps, each a little more enclosed:

- **Doors that overlap the pilaster,** so there is no sightline at the door edges.
- **Taller doors and panels, mounted closer to the floor:** 64" to 72" doors in place of the standard 58".
- **Full-height stalls** that run from the floor to the ceiling.

Taller doors aren't offered in every mounting style, and the accessible stall has to keep its toe clearance, so decide the privacy level before the layout is final. The options are compared in [why do bathroom stalls have gaps](/blog/why-do-bathroom-stalls-have-gaps), and the heights in [how tall are bathroom stalls](/blog/how-tall-are-bathroom-stalls).

## Choose finishes for how the room is cleaned

![Five partition doors cut through and seen edge on: phenolic, solid plastic, plastic laminate, powder coated steel, stainless steel](/images/diagrams/material-sections-compare.svg)

| Material | The look | Where it works |
|---|---|---|
| [Plastic laminate](/partitions/plastic-laminate) | The widest choice of colors and patterns | Dry rooms where the stalls should match the millwork |
| [Stainless steel](/partitions/stainless-steel) | Brushed or textured metal | Premium rooms |
| [Phenolic](/partitions/phenolic) | Slim, hard panels | Busy and wet rooms |
| [Solid plastic (HDPE)](/partitions/solid-plastic-hdpe) | Solid panels, color all the way through | Rooms that are hosed down |
| [Powder coated steel](/partitions/powder-coated-steel) | A clean painted finish in a wide range of colors | Dry rooms on a budget |

The finish has to survive the cleaning routine. Laminate swells where water reaches its core, and painted steel rusts where its coating is damaged, so a room that is hosed down needs phenolic or solid plastic.

## Pick the mounting style for the look and the building

![The four mounting styles side by side: overhead braced, floor mounted, ceiling hung and floor to ceiling](/images/diagrams/mounting-styles-compare.svg)

- **[Ceiling hung](/mounting-styles/ceiling-hung)** leaves the floor clear, which looks light and cleans fast. It needs structural steel above the ceiling.
- **[Floor mounted](/mounting-styles/floor-mounted)** has no headrail, for a clean line across the tops. It needs structural concrete.
- **[Floor to ceiling](/mounting-styles/floor-to-ceiling)** suits tall, private doors.
- **[Overhead braced](/mounting-styles/overhead-braced)** works in the most buildings.

The structure decides which of these you can have, so check the floor and ceiling before you design around a style.

## Build accessibility into the first sketch

![Plan of a wheelchair accessible toilet compartment at minimum size, with the minimum area, toilet centerline, grab bars, door clear width and door location](/images/diagrams/ada-wheelchair-stall.svg)

- Where there are stalls, at least one must be wheelchair accessible: at least 60" wide, and 56" or 59" deep depending on the toilet.
- Six or more stalls, or six or more toilets and urinals combined, also need an ambulatory accessible stall.
- The accessible stall's door needs 32" of clear opening and can't swing into the stall's minimum area.

Placed at the end of the row, the accessible stall uses the side wall for its grab bar and keeps its outswing door clear of the entry. The rules are in our [ADA compliance guide](/guides/ada-compliance).

## FAQ

### What makes a commercial bathroom design modern?
Privacy without gaps, finishes that match the rest of the interior, a clear floor and an accessible stall designed in from the start.

### How wide should a commercial bathroom stall be?
A standard stall is typically 36" wide. The plumbing code minimum is 30", and a wheelchair accessible stall is at least 60" wide.

### Which stall material looks most upscale?
Stainless steel and phenolic. Plastic laminate offers the widest choice of colors and patterns for dry rooms.

### How do I get bathroom stalls with no gaps?
Choose doors that overlap the pilaster, taller doors mounted closer to the floor, or full-height stalls that run from floor to ceiling.

### Which mounting style looks cleanest?
Ceiling hung stalls leave the floor clear, and floor mounted stalls have no headrail. Each needs the right structure: steel above the ceiling, or structural concrete.
