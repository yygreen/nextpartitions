## The right material for Tucson's climate

Tucson gets about 11 inches of rain a year, on roughly 51 days, and relative humidity averages 47% in the morning and 24% in the afternoon. The weather rarely decides the material here. The room does:

- **Most restrooms** (offices, restaurants, retail, clinics, schools): all five materials work. Choose on looks, cleaning routine and budget.
- **Rooms that get wet from the inside** (pool facilities, gym locker rooms, restrooms that are hosed down): choose [solid plastic (HDPE)](/partitions/solid-plastic-hdpe) or [phenolic](/partitions/phenolic). Both are solid all the way through and handle constant moisture.

[Plastic laminate](/partitions/plastic-laminate) has a particleboard core that may swell with excessive moisture, and [powder coated steel](/partitions/powder-coated-steel) can rust where the coating is damaged, so keep both for dry rooms. [Stainless steel](/partitions/stainless-steel) resists corrosion and suits premium public restrooms. Compare all five on the [partitions page](/partitions).

## The rules a Tucson restroom has to meet

- **Wheelchair access:** a restroom with stalls needs at least one stall a wheelchair user can enter and use. Arizona's disability law uses the federal sizes, and state law writes them into every local building code, so the city or county building department checks them.
- **Fire safety:** Tucson's building rules require solid plastic (HDPE) partitions to pass a fire test. Ask for the fire-rated version.
- **How many stalls:** Tucson sets the number of toilets by how many people use the building, and caps how many of the men's toilets may be urinals. The table below shows what that means.
- **Arizona and federal stall rules match:** Arizona adopts the federal standards for stalls, doors and grab bars, and state law says its rules may not go beyond them. A city may set stricter rules of its own.
- **Tucson access rules:** alongside the federal standard, Tucson has adopted a national accessibility standard for buildings, in its 2017 edition.
- **Single-user and all-gender restrooms:** under Tucson's plumbing rules a one-person restroom must be marked as open to everyone. A shared all-gender room may take the place of separate men's and women's rooms if every toilet sits in its own compartment with a door and every urinal has its own partitioned area. Where a row of one-person rooms serves everyone, Tucson lets the rooms share sinks placed directly outside them, as long as the number of sinks is not reduced. A measure Arizona voters approved in November 2026 says that from January 1, 2027, a school or athletic association may not authorize anyone to use a restroom, locker room or shower room that is part of its athletics facilities and is not designated for that person's sex.
- **Permits:** Tucson requires a permit to alter a building unless the work is on its exempt list, and the city says commercial tenant improvements need one. The list covers painting, tiling, carpeting, cabinets, counter tops and similar finish work, and "nonfixed and movable fixtures, cases, racks, counters and partitions not over 5 feet 9 inches" high, but it doesn't name toilet partitions. Ask Tucson Planning and Development Services whether your replacement needs one. You don't need a permit to buy partitions.

Rules change, so confirm your project with Tucson Planning and Development Services or your architect. The accessible stall's sizes are in our [ADA compliance guide](/guides/ada-compliance), and fixture spacing is in the [restroom layout guide](/guides/restroom-layout).

## How many stalls a Tucson restroom needs

Arizona has no statewide building rules, so Tucson's own rules decide. Tucson counts toilets by a building's capacity: the most people it is designed to hold at one time, worked out from its floor area. It isn't the number of staff or of visitors in a day. Half are counted as men and half as women. Every toilet needs a stall. Urinals are optional: in the men's room they may take the place of up to half of the toilets, or up to two thirds in restaurants and schools, so the table shows men's toilets as stalls.

:::stalls
head: Office | Restaurant | School
50: 1/1 | 1/1 | 1/1
100: 2/2 | 1/1 | 1/1
200: 3/3 | 2/2 | 2/2
400: 5/5 | 3/3 | 4/4
:::

These are minimums for the whole building, and at least one stall in each room must be wheelchair accessible. An office designed for 25 people or fewer, a shop designed for 100 or fewer, and any building designed for 15 or fewer, doesn't need separate men's and women's rooms. Confirm the count with Tucson Planning and Development Services.

To find your building's capacity, Tucson's building rules count an office at one person per 150 square feet of floor, a restaurant dining room at one per 15 square feet and a classroom at one per 20. Your architect or Tucson Planning and Development Services sets the final number.

## What installation labor and sales tax add in Tucson

- **Local labor rates:** carpenters in the Tucson area earn a median of $23.61 an hour, about 19% below the national median of $29.12. It's a guide to local labor rates, not a quote: installers set their own prices.
- **Public jobs:** Arizona has no wage law for public jobs, and state law bars cities, counties and school districts from requiring prevailing wages in their contracts. On federally funded work the federal rate applies: $33.19 an hour plus $19.20 in benefits for a carpenter on building work in Pima County.
- **Sales tax:** 8.7% in Tucson: 5.6% state, 0.5% Pima County and 2.6% city.
- **Tax on installation:** in Arizona, a contractor's labor to repair, replace or alter part of an existing building is not taxed. The contractor pays tax on the materials and adds no tax line to the invoice. New construction, and alterations to a commercial building under a contract above $750,000, are taxed as contracting on 65% of the contract price.
- **Buying without installation:** partitions shipped to Tucson are taxable. A delivery charge listed separately on the invoice is not. An out-of-state seller with more than $100,000 a year in Arizona sales collects the tax. If the seller doesn't collect it, the buyer owes use tax to the state.

How tax applies depends on your contract, so confirm with your accountant or the Arizona Department of Revenue.
