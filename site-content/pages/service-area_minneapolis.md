## The right material for the Minneapolis climate

Minneapolis gets about 32 inches of rain a year, on roughly 115 days, and relative humidity averages 79% in the morning and 59% in the afternoon. On average the wettest month is June (about 4.6 inches) and the driest is February (about 0.9 inches). What that means for partitions depends on whether the restroom is heated and ventilated:

- **Heated, ventilated restrooms** (offices, restaurants, clinics, most schools): all five materials work. Choose on looks, cleaning routine and budget.
- **Unheated restrooms or rooms open to outside air** (parks, stadiums, warehouses, pool and locker rooms): choose [solid plastic (HDPE)](/partitions/solid-plastic-hdpe) or [phenolic](/partitions/phenolic). Both are solid all the way through and handle constant moisture.

Keep [plastic laminate](/partitions/plastic-laminate) and [powder coated steel](/partitions/powder-coated-steel) for dry rooms; [stainless steel](/partitions/stainless-steel) resists corrosion. Compare all five on the [partitions page](/partitions).

## The rules a Minneapolis restroom has to meet

- **Wheelchair access:** a restroom with stalls needs at least one stall a wheelchair user can enter and use. Federal law and Minnesota's own access rules set the sizes. Minnesota has no separate state sign-off for access: your local building department checks it with the permit, and the state reviews the plans itself for state-funded buildings, public schools and state-licensed buildings such as hospitals.
- **Fire safety:** Minnesota's building rules treat toilet partitions as an interior finish and require solid plastic (HDPE) and polypropylene partitions to pass a room-corner fire test. Ask for the fire-rated version.
- **How many stalls:** Minnesota's building rules set the number of toilets by how many people use the building. Minneapolis enforces them as the state writes them, because cities may not change the state building rules. The table below shows what that means.
- **Minnesota extras:** Minnesota goes further than the federal rules in four places. A restroom with two or more stalls needs a second, narrower accessible stall with grab bars on both sides, where the federal rule asks for one only from six toilets and urinals. The wheelchair stall is deeper: 78 inches, or 48 inches plus the depth of the toilet, against 56 to 59 inches federally. Its side wall needs an 18 inch vertical grab bar as well as the horizontal one. And a baby changing table may not sit inside a stall.
- **Single-user and all-gender restrooms:** a one-person restroom must be marked for use by either sex. Minnesota's building rules call for separate men's and women's rooms once a space holds more than 25 people, and they don't yet set out how a shared all-gender room with stalls can take their place, so ask Minneapolis Construction Code Services before planning one. No Minnesota law limits who may use which restroom.
- **Permits:** Minneapolis says almost all projects need a building permit before work starts. The work that needs none is listed in the state building rules, which the city points to. That list includes "painting, papering, tiling, carpeting, cabinets, countertops, and similar finish work" and "movable cases, counters, and partitions not over five feet, nine inches" in height. It doesn't name toilet partitions, and neither do the city's own examples of exempt interior work. Ask Minneapolis Construction Code Services (612-673-3000) whether your replacement needs one. You don't need a permit to buy partitions.

Rules change, so confirm your project with Minneapolis Construction Code Services or your architect. The accessible stall's sizes are in our [ADA compliance guide](/guides/ada-compliance), and fixture spacing is in the [restroom layout guide](/guides/restroom-layout).

## How many stalls a Minneapolis restroom needs

Minnesota's rules count toilets by a building's capacity: the most people it is designed to hold at one time, worked out from its floor area. Half are counted as men and half as women, and any fraction rounds up. Every toilet needs a stall. Urinals are optional: in each men's room they may take the place of up to two thirds of the required toilets, and each urinal needs a screen.

:::stalls
head: Office | Restaurant | School
50: 1/1 | 1/1 | 1/1
100: 2/2 | 1/1 | 1/1
200: 3/3 | 2/2 | 2/2
400: 5/5 | 3/3 | 4/4
:::

These are minimums for the whole building, and the men's figures show toilets only, before any are swapped for urinals. A space that holds 25 people or fewer can use one shared restroom. The same rules apply in every Minnesota city, because cities may not change the state building rules. At least one stall in each room must be wheelchair accessible.

To find your building's capacity, the building rules count an office at one person per 150 square feet of floor, a restaurant dining room at one per 15 square feet and a classroom at one per 20. Your architect or Minneapolis Construction Code Services sets the final number.

## What installation labor and sales tax add in Minneapolis

- **Local labor rates:** carpenters in the Minneapolis–St. Paul–Bloomington area earn a median of $35.61 an hour, about 22% above the national median of $29.12. It's a guide to local labor rates, not a quote: installers set their own prices.
- **Public jobs:** on projects paid for in whole or in part with state money, including state buildings, colleges and schools, Minnesota law requires at least the wage the state certifies for each trade in each county, once the job costs $2,500 with one trade or $25,000 with several. For a carpenter on commercial building work in Hennepin County that is $51.56 an hour plus $29.84 in benefits. Minneapolis has its own rule too: contractors on city construction contracts of $50,000 or more must pay the prevailing wage.
- **Sales tax:** 9.025% in Minneapolis: 6.875% state, 0.5% city, 0.15% Hennepin County, 0.5% county transit and 1% in two metro area taxes (0.75% for transportation and 0.25% for housing).
- **Tax on installation:** in Minnesota, labor to permanently attach an item to a building is not taxed, and a contractor who supplies and installs items that become part of the building doesn't charge sales tax on the job, paying it on the materials instead. Where an installed item stays the owner's movable property, the item and the labor to install it are both taxed, even on separate invoices. The Department of Revenue's guides don't say which applies to toilet partitions, so ask before you budget.
- **Buying without installation:** partitions shipped to Minneapolis are taxable, freight included. If the seller doesn't collect the tax, the buyer owes it to the state as use tax at the same rate.

How tax applies depends on your contract, so confirm with your accountant or the Minnesota Department of Revenue.
