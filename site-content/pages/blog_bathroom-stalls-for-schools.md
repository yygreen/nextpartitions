# How to Pick the Best Bathroom Stalls for Schools

The best bathroom stalls for schools survive kicks, graffiti, hanging on doors and daily disinfecting, and still pass the fire and accessibility rules a school building has to meet. That narrows the choice quickly: two materials, two mounting styles, and a handful of details that decide whether the stalls last.

## Five things to decide

![Front view of a run of stalls with five things marked: the door surface, the headrail, a full-height hinge, the gaps at a door's edges and the space under a door](/images/diagrams/school-stall-decisions.svg)

1. The material: what the surface is made of
2. The mounting style: what holds the stalls up
3. The hardware: hinges that survive slamming
4. Privacy: the gaps at the door edges
5. Floor clearance in the accessible stall

## Start with the material: HDPE or phenolic

Five materials are sold for toilet partitions. Seen edge on, they're built very differently:

![Five partition doors cut through and seen edge on: phenolic, solid plastic, plastic laminate, powder coated steel, stainless steel](/images/diagrams/material-sections-compare.svg)

For school restrooms, two of them do most of the work, because they are solid all the way through and don't mind water:

| | Solid plastic (HDPE) | Phenolic |
|---|---|---|
| Build | 1" solid plastic, color all the way through | Compact laminate: 1/2" panels with 3/4" doors and pilasters; black core standard, color-through on some lines |
| Rough use | Won't easily dent, rust or delaminate; the surface scratches more easily, but a scratch shows the same color | Hard surface; resists dents and scratches. A deep scratch on black core can show the core |
| Graffiti | Usually wipes off with a nonabrasive cleaner; xylene-based markers can leave a shadow | Resists graffiti |
| Water | Can be hosed down | Handles constant moisture |
| Fire | Must pass NFPA 286: ask for the fire-rated version | Tested to ASTM E84 and classed A, B or C |
| Published warranties | 15 to 25 years | 3 to 25 years; many lines 25 |

Our full comparison is in [HDPE vs phenolic partitions](/blog/hdpe-vs-phenolic-partitions). The short version: [HDPE](/partitions/solid-plastic-hdpe) is the usual first choice for a school on a budget, and [phenolic](/partitions/phenolic) earns its higher price where dents and scratches are the main problem or the fire code asks for a Class A material.

What about the other three? [Powder coated steel](/partitions/powder-coated-steel) costs the least to buy, but painted steel can rust where the coating is damaged, and graffiti is harder to remove from it. [Plastic laminate](/partitions/plastic-laminate) has a particleboard core that may swell with moisture. Both suit dry, gently used rooms, such as a staff restroom. [Stainless steel](/partitions/stainless-steel) is durable and easy to sanitize, at a premium price.

## The fire rule that catches schools out

This is the detail to get right before anything is ordered. The International Building Code requires HDPE used as an interior finish, which includes toilet partitions, to pass the NFPA 286 room-corner fire test. Not every HDPE color passes: standard color collections may not meet NFPA 286. A school can't simply pick a color from a standard chart.

- Ask for the **fire-rated version** of the HDPE line, and for its test report.
- Expect a shorter color list, and sometimes a higher price, for the fire-rated colors.
- Confirm with your architect or code official which edition of the code applies to your building.

The details are on our [HDPE page](/partitions/solid-plastic-hdpe).

## Choose the mounting style for rough use

The mounting style decides how the stalls are held up, and how well they stand up to pushing, pulling and climbing:

![The four mounting styles side by side: overhead braced, floor mounted, ceiling hung and floor to ceiling](/images/diagrams/mounting-styles-compare.svg)

- **[Overhead braced](/mounting-styles/overhead-braced)** is the standard for schools. Pilasters are anchored to the floor and tied together by a headrail across the top. It asks the least of the building and works with any ceiling.
- **[Floor to ceiling](/mounting-styles/floor-to-ceiling)** anchors every pilaster at both ends, for the rooms that take the most abuse. It needs a concrete floor and structure at the ceiling.
- **Floor mounted and ceiling hung** are each held at one end only. They suit calmer rooms better than a student restroom.

Our [mounting style comparison](/blog/ceiling-hung-vs-floor-mounted-vs-floor-to-ceiling) explains what each one needs from the floor and ceiling.

## Hardware that survives students

Doors get slammed and swung on, so the hinges matter as much as the panels:

![Gravity (cam), wrap-around and continuous bathroom stall hinges](/images/diagrams/hinge-types.svg)

- **Continuous hinges** run the full height of the door. They spread the load along the whole door edge and close the gap at the hinge side.
- **Wrap-around hinges** are surface-mounted and through-bolted for vandal resistance.
- **The accessible stall's door must be self-closing.** Check that whichever hinge you choose does this.

One caution: a gravity-type continuous hinge can let a door slam, and a continuous spring hinge may not work with emergency access. Ask which version a line uses. More on the [hinges page](/hardware/hinges).

## Heights by age group

Standard doors and panels are 55" or 58" high and sit about 12" to 14" above the floor, depending on the manufacturer. That is what most secondary schools use. Elementary restrooms often use lower doors and panels. Heights vary by material and manufacturer, so confirm what is available for the line you choose before you plan around a number.

## Privacy

Students notice gaps. A standard stall has four of them:

![The four gaps in a standard bathroom stall, front view](/images/diagrams/stall-gaps.svg)

Replacement or new construction is the easiest time to close them. Options include doors that overlap the pilaster so there is no sightline, continuous hinges and brackets, and taller doors mounted closer to the floor. Taller doors don't work with every mounting style; some lines offer them only overhead braced or floor to ceiling. The options and their limits are in [why do bathroom stalls have gaps](/blog/why-do-bathroom-stalls-have-gaps).

## Accessibility: the rules change for children

The accessible stall in a school has two sets of rules to check.

**How many.** Where a restroom has toilet compartments, at least one must be wheelchair accessible. Add an ambulatory accessible compartment (35" to 37" wide) once the room has six or more compartments, or six or more toilets and urinals combined. A boys' room with four stalls and two urinals needs one.

**Children's dimensions.** Where an accessible compartment is designed for children (ages 3 to 12), the 2010 ADA Standards set its size at 60" wide by 59" deep minimum, and its toe clearance at 12" instead of the usual 9":

![Front partition, side partition and a section through a partition, with the 9-inch toe clearance under the doors and panels](/images/diagrams/ada-toe-clearance.svg)

The drawing shows the standard 9" clearance; for a children's compartment, read 12". This matters for privacy: if you lower the panels to close the floor gap, the accessible stall's front and one side partition must still keep that clearance, unless the compartment is larger than the exception sizes. Decide it before you order. The full rules are in our [ADA compliance guide](/guides/ada-compliance).

## Plan around the school calendar

Most schools replace stalls when the building is empty. Partitions are made to order from approved shop drawings, so work back from the break: measure, get the quote, approve the drawings, allow for manufacturing and shipping, then book the installer. If you have several restrooms, measure them all at once and quote them together.

## FAQ

### What is the best material for school bathroom stalls?
Solid plastic (HDPE) or phenolic. Both are solid through, resist water and stand up to rough use. HDPE costs less; phenolic resists dents and scratches better.

### Do school bathroom partitions need a fire rating?
HDPE partitions must pass the NFPA 286 fire test under the International Building Code, and standard colors may not. Ask for the fire-rated version and its test report. Other materials are classed under ASTM E84.

### Which mounting style is best for schools?
Overhead braced is the standard. Floor to ceiling anchors each pilaster at both ends, for rooms that take the most abuse.

### How many ADA stalls does a school restroom need?
At least one wheelchair accessible compartment where there are toilet compartments, plus an ambulatory one where there are six or more compartments, or six or more toilets and urinals combined.

### Are bathroom stalls smaller in elementary schools?
Doors and panels are often lower. Accessible compartments designed for children ages 3 to 12 have their own dimensions and need 12" of toe clearance.

### Can graffiti be removed from school bathroom stalls?
Usually, with a nonabrasive cleaner on HDPE or phenolic. Xylene-based markers can leave a shadow on HDPE.
