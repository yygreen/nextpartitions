## The right material for Philadelphia's climate

Philadelphia gets about 44 inches of rain a year, on roughly 118 days, and relative humidity averages 76% in the morning and 54% in the afternoon. On average the wettest month is September (about 4.4 inches) and the driest is February (about 2.8 inches). What that means for partitions depends on whether the restroom is heated and ventilated:

- **Heated, ventilated restrooms** (offices, restaurants, clinics, most schools): all five materials work. Choose on looks, cleaning routine and budget.
- **Unheated restrooms or rooms open to outside air** (parks, stadiums, warehouses, pool and locker rooms): choose [solid plastic (HDPE)](/partitions/solid-plastic-hdpe) or [phenolic](/partitions/phenolic). Both are solid all the way through and handle constant moisture.

Keep [plastic laminate](/partitions/plastic-laminate) and [powder coated steel](/partitions/powder-coated-steel) for dry rooms; [stainless steel](/partitions/stainless-steel) resists corrosion. Compare all five on the [partitions page](/partitions).

## The rules a Philadelphia restroom has to meet

- **Wheelchair access:** a restroom with stalls needs at least one stall a wheelchair user can enter and use, and a room with six or more toilets and urinals also needs a narrower stall with grab bars on both sides. Federal law and Pennsylvania's building rules set the sizes. Access is checked with the building permit, by your local code office or, where it has no certified access inspector, by the state Department of Labor & Industry. Only the state can approve an exception.
- **Fire safety:** Pennsylvania's building rules treat toilet partitions as an interior finish and require solid plastic (HDPE) and polypropylene partitions to pass a room-corner fire test. Ask for the fire-rated version.
- **How many stalls:** Philadelphia's plumbing rules set the number of toilets by how many people use the building, with the same numbers as the rest of Pennsylvania. The table below shows what that means.
- **Pennsylvania extras:** Pennsylvania goes further than the federal rules in three places. The accessible stall needs an 18 inch vertical grab bar on the side wall as well as the horizontal one. In a very large restroom, 5% of the stalls must be wheelchair accessible, which means a second one from 21 stalls up. And a restaurant or store that needs six or more toilets in total must add a one-toilet family restroom.
- **Single-user and all-gender restrooms:** a one-person restroom must be marked as open to everyone. A separate city law requires gender-neutral signs on single-occupancy restrooms in buildings open to the public, shops and City-owned buildings included, and it covers existing restrooms as well as new ones. Philadelphia allows a shared all-gender room where the required number of toilets is the same for men and women, and sets its own stall rule for it: the partitions and doors around every toilet must run from floor to ceiling, and urinals aren't allowed in the room. We found no Pennsylvania law that limits who may use which restroom.
- **Permits:** Philadelphia requires a building permit for work that changes the interior of a building. Its list of work that needs none includes ordinary repairs, painting, cabinets, countertops and similar finishing work, and movable partitions no taller than 5 feet 9 inches. It doesn't name toilet partitions fixed to the floor or wall, so ask the Department of Licenses and Inspections (311, or 215-686-8686 from outside the city) whether your replacement needs one. The exemptions are for properties that are not on the Philadelphia Historic Registry. You don't need a permit to buy partitions.

Rules change, so confirm your project with the Philadelphia Department of Licenses and Inspections (L&I) or your architect. The accessible stall's sizes are in our [ADA compliance guide](/guides/ada-compliance), and fixture spacing is in the [restroom layout guide](/guides/restroom-layout).

## How many stalls a Philadelphia restroom needs

Philadelphia takes its toilet counts from its own plumbing rules, and the numbers match the rest of Pennsylvania. They are based on a building's capacity: the most people it is designed to hold at one time, worked out from its floor area. Half are counted as men and half as women, and any fraction rounds up. Every toilet needs a stall. Urinals are optional: in each men's room they may take the place of up to half of the required toilets in an office, and up to two thirds in a restaurant or school, and each urinal needs a screen.

:::stalls
head: Office | Restaurant | School
50: 1/1 | 1/1 | 1/1
100: 2/2 | 1/1 | 1/1
200: 3/3 | 2/2 | 2/2
400: 5/5 | 3/3 | 4/4
:::

These are minimums for the whole building, and the men's figures show toilets only, before any are swapped for urinals. An office with 25 people or fewer, or any space with 15 or fewer, can use one shared restroom. A shared all-gender room is counted on everyone in the building instead of half. In Philadelphia it can't have urinals, so every fixture in it is a toilet behind floor-to-ceiling partitions and a full-height door. A separate Pennsylvania law asks for two women's toilets for every men's toilet or urinal in new or largely rebuilt public middle and high schools, arenas, stadiums and convention halls, so ask L&I whether it covers your project. At least one stall in each room must be wheelchair accessible.

To find your building's capacity, the building rules count an office at one person per 150 square feet of floor, a restaurant dining room at one per 15 square feet and a classroom at one per 20. Your architect or L&I sets the final number.

## What installation labor and sales tax add in Philadelphia

- **Local labor rates:** carpenters in the Philadelphia–Camden–Wilmington area earn a median of $29.70 an hour, about 2% above the national median of $29.12. It's a guide to local labor rates, not a quote: installers set their own prices.
- **Public jobs:** on projects paid for in whole or in part with public money and costing more than $25,000, school district work included, Pennsylvania law requires at least the wage the state Department of Labor & Industry sets for each trade in the county; maintenance work is not covered. The state issues its rates project by project. On federally funded building work in Philadelphia County the carpenter rate is $57.72 an hour plus $30.39 in benefits. Philadelphia has its own wage rule for City construction contracts over $2,000: the City's schedule sets $57.72 an hour plus $30.89 in benefits for a carpenter.
- **Sales tax:** 8% in Philadelphia: 6% state and 2% city. A seller located outside Philadelphia isn't required to collect the 2% city part. Where it doesn't, the buyer owes that part to the state as use tax.
- **Tax on installation:** in Pennsylvania, a contractor who supplies and installs partitions that are bolted or screwed to the building doesn't charge sales tax on the job, and pays the tax on the materials instead. State tax rules presume that partitions fixed this way become part of the building. They don't spell out the case of an installer fitting partitions you bought yourself, so ask.
- **Buying without installation:** partitions shipped to Philadelphia are taxable, and so is delivery charged by the seller. If the seller doesn't collect the tax, the buyer owes it to the state.

How tax applies depends on your contract, so confirm with your accountant or the Pennsylvania Department of Revenue.
