## The right material for Portland's climate

Portland gets about 37 inches of rain a year, on roughly 153 days, and relative humidity averages 82% in the morning and 59% in the afternoon. What that means for partitions depends on whether the restroom is heated and ventilated:

- **Heated, ventilated restrooms** (offices, restaurants, clinics, most schools): all five materials work. Choose on looks, cleaning routine and budget.
- **Unheated restrooms or rooms open to outside air** (parks, stadiums, warehouses, pool and locker rooms): choose [solid plastic (HDPE)](/partitions/solid-plastic-hdpe) or [phenolic](/partitions/phenolic). Both are solid all the way through and handle constant moisture.

[Plastic laminate](/partitions/plastic-laminate) has a particleboard core that may swell with excessive moisture, and [powder coated steel](/partitions/powder-coated-steel) can rust where the coating is damaged, so keep both for dry rooms. [Stainless steel](/partitions/stainless-steel) resists corrosion and suits premium public restrooms. Compare all five on the [partitions page](/partitions).

## The rules a Portland restroom has to meet

- **Wheelchair access:** a restroom with stalls needs at least one stall a wheelchair user can enter and use, and a room with six or more toilets and urinals also needs a narrower stall with grab bars on both sides. Federal law and Oregon's building rules set the sizes. Oregon has no separate state sign-off for access: your city or county building department checks it when it reviews the plans. When you remodel, the restrooms serving the remodeled area have to be made accessible too, up to a quarter of the remodel's cost.
- **Fire safety:** Oregon's building rules treat toilet partitions as an interior finish and require solid plastic (HDPE) and polypropylene partitions to pass a room-corner fire test. Ask for the fire-rated version.
- **How many stalls:** Oregon's building rules set the number of toilets by how many people use the building, and they are the same in every Oregon city. Portland enforces them without changes of its own. The table below shows what that means.
- **Oregon extras:** Oregon goes further than the federal rules in three places. The accessible stall needs an 18 inch vertical grab bar on the side wall as well as the horizontal one. Its partitions need 12 inches of toe space above the floor, where the federal rule asks for 9. And in a very large restroom, 5% of the stalls must be wheelchair accessible, which means a second one from 21 stalls up.
- **Shared all-gender rooms in Portland:** Portland's building official has set out what the state's privacy rule means in a shared all-gender restroom. Each toilet must be enclosed from floor to ceiling. The door may start no more than 1 inch above the floor and must reach at least 84 inches above it, the frame must overlap the door so nobody can see in, and the lock must show whether the stall is occupied. Each enclosure needs its own ventilation. A urinal in that room needs the same kind of enclosure. Tell us in your quote request if the room is all-gender.
- **Single-user and all-gender restrooms:** a one-person restroom must be marked as open to everyone, and its door needs a privacy lock that shows when the room is occupied. A Portland ordinance also covers existing one-person restrooms in places open to the public: signs that limit the room to one gender must be replaced by January 1, 2027, and the city enforces the rule from July 1, 2027. Oregon lets a building meet its toilet count with shared all-gender rooms or with separate men's and women's rooms. Either way, each toilet sits in its own compartment with a door and each urinal has a privacy screen. Oregon law bars discrimination by sex or gender identity in places open to the public and in public schools.
- **Permits:** Portland Permitting & Development issues building permits in Portland. Its list of commercial work that needs a permit includes tenant improvements, disabled access upgrades and moving or building walls, non-bearing partitions included. Oregon's building rules exempt finish work such as painting, tiling and cabinets, and movable partitions no taller than 5 feet 9 inches, such as office cubicles. Neither list names toilet partitions. Ask Portland Permitting & Development (503-823-7300) whether your replacement needs one. You don't need a permit to buy partitions.

Rules change, so confirm your project with Portland Permitting & Development or your architect. The accessible stall's sizes are in our [ADA compliance guide](/guides/ada-compliance), and fixture spacing is in the [restroom layout guide](/guides/restroom-layout).

## How many stalls a Portland restroom needs

Oregon's rules count toilets by a building's capacity: the most people it is designed to hold at one time, worked out from its floor area. Half are counted as men and half as women, and any fraction rounds up. Every toilet needs a stall with a door. Urinals are optional: in each men's room they may take the place of up to two thirds of the required toilets, and each urinal needs a privacy screen.

:::stalls
head: Office | Restaurant | School
50: 1/1 | 1/1 | 1/1
100: 2/2 | 1/1 | 1/1
200: 3/3 | 2/2 | 2/2
400: 5/5 | 3/3 | 4/4
:::

These are minimums, and the men's figures show toilets only, before any are swapped for urinals. An office with 50 people or fewer, or any other space with 30 or fewer, can use a single one-person restroom instead. Where a shared restroom is built for all genders, the count is taken on everyone together rather than half and half, and in Portland urinals in that room don't count toward it. At least one stall in each room must be wheelchair accessible.

To find your building's capacity, Oregon's building rules count an office at one person per 150 square feet of floor, a restaurant dining room at one per 15 square feet and a classroom at one per 20. For an office's toilet count, Portland counts conference rooms, break rooms and shared work areas larger than 750 square feet at one person per 15 square feet.

## What installation labor and sales tax add in Portland

- **Local labor rates:** carpenters in the Portland–Vancouver–Hillsboro area earn a median of $35.44 an hour, about 22% above the national median of $29.12. It's a guide to local labor rates, not a quote: installers set their own prices.
- **Public jobs:** on public projects over $50,000, school district and city buildings included, Oregon law requires at least the wage the state's Bureau of Labor and Industries sets for each trade. For a carpenter on a job in Portland that is $56.69 an hour plus $17.81 in benefits.
- **Sales tax:** none in Portland. Oregon has no general sales tax or use tax.
- **Tax on installation:** Oregon has no general sales tax, so none is added to installation labor or to a contractor's supply-and-install price.
- **Buying without installation:** partitions shipped to Portland carry no Oregon sales tax or use tax, on the partitions or on the freight.

How tax applies depends on your contract, so confirm with your accountant or the Oregon Department of Revenue.
