## The right material for San Francisco's climate

San Francisco gets about 23 inches of rain a year, on roughly 67 days, and relative humidity averages 87% in the morning and 69% in the afternoon. On average the wettest month is December (about 4.8 inches) and the driest is July (almost none). In most San Francisco restrooms the room decides the material, not the weather:

- **Air-conditioned restrooms** (offices, restaurants, clinics, most schools): all five materials work. Choose on looks, cleaning routine and budget.
- **Restrooms without air conditioning, or that get wet from the inside** (warehouses, plants, parks, stadiums, pool and locker rooms): choose [solid plastic (HDPE)](/partitions/solid-plastic-hdpe) or [phenolic](/partitions/phenolic). Both are solid all the way through and handle constant moisture.

Keep [plastic laminate](/partitions/plastic-laminate) and [powder coated steel](/partitions/powder-coated-steel) for dry rooms; [stainless steel](/partitions/stainless-steel) resists corrosion. Compare all five on the [partitions page](/partitions).

## The rules a San Francisco restroom has to meet

- **Wheelchair access:** a restroom with stalls needs at least one stall a wheelchair user can enter and use. Federal law and California's own access rules both set the sizes, and California's are stricter in places. A remodel can also oblige you to bring the restrooms serving that area up to today's rules; on projects under $209,208 (the 2026 figure) that duty is limited.
- **Fire safety:** building rules require solid plastic (HDPE) partitions to pass a fire test. Ask for the fire-rated version.
- **How many stalls:** San Francisco uses California's rules, which set the number of toilets and urinals by how many people use the building. The table below shows what that means.
- **California extras:** California asks more of a stall than the federal rules. In a large restroom, 5% of the stalls must be wheelchair accessible, not just one. The accessible stall needs more open floor in front of the toilet, so it's deeper, and the aisle outside its door must be 48 inches wide, against 42. Stall doors need a privacy latch, and every restroom door carries California's circle or triangle symbol. Give your layout to your architect before you order.
- **Single-user and all-gender restrooms:** a one-person restroom must be signed as all-gender. San Francisco's own ordinance requires the same of every one-person restroom open to the public or to employees, and the Department of Building Inspection checks the signs during inspections. A shared all-gender room is allowed in place of separate men's and women's rooms if every toilet is in a floor-to-ceiling privacy compartment whose door locks from the inside and shows when it's occupied. Buildings on land the city owns or leases need at least one all-gender restroom on each floor when they are built or extensively renovated.
- **Permits:** San Francisco's list of work that needs no building permit includes "movable cases, counters and partitions not over 5 feet 9 inches (1753 mm) high" and "painting, papering and similar finish work". It doesn't name toilet partitions, so ask the Department of Building Inspection (628-652-3200) whether your replacement needs one. You don't need a permit to buy partitions.

Rules change, so confirm your project with the San Francisco Department of Building Inspection or your architect. The accessible stall's sizes are in our [ADA compliance guide](/guides/ada-compliance), and fixture spacing is in the [restroom layout guide](/guides/restroom-layout).

## How many stalls a San Francisco restroom needs

San Francisco counts toilets by a building's capacity: the most people it is designed to hold at one time, worked out from its floor area. It isn't the number of staff or of visitors in a day. Half are counted as men and half as women, and California's rules require urinals in the men's room. Every toilet needs a stall, and urinals need screens between them. The women's room must have at least as many toilets as the men's room has toilets and urinals together.

:::stalls
head: Office | Restaurant | School
50: 2/1+1 | 2/1+1 | 2/1+1
100: 3/1+1 | 2/1+1 | 2/1+1
200: 4/2+1 | 3/2+1 | 4/2+1
400: 8/3+2 | 4/3+1 | 7/4+2
:::

San Francisco applies these minimums to new buildings, first-time tenant build-outs, tenant expansions, changes of use and additions. Other projects don't have to recalculate the number of toilets. An office or shop with 50 people or fewer may have a single one-person restroom. At least one stall in each room must be wheelchair accessible.

To find your building's capacity, San Francisco's plan checkers use the building code's floor-area figures. Your architect can ask to use the plumbing code's figures instead: one person per 150 square feet of office floor, and one per 30 square feet of restaurant dining room or classroom. Your architect or the San Francisco Department of Building Inspection sets the final number.
