## The right material for Mesa's climate

The nearest long-term weather station, at Phoenix, records about 7 inches of rain a year, on roughly 35 days, and relative humidity averages 46% in the morning and 23% in the afternoon. The weather rarely decides the material here. The room does:

- **Most restrooms** (offices, restaurants, retail, clinics, schools): all five materials work. Choose on looks, cleaning routine and budget.
- **Rooms that get wet from the inside** (pool facilities, gym locker rooms, restrooms that are hosed down): choose [solid plastic (HDPE)](/partitions/solid-plastic-hdpe) or [phenolic](/partitions/phenolic). Both are solid all the way through and handle constant moisture.

[Plastic laminate](/partitions/plastic-laminate) has a particleboard core that may swell with excessive moisture, and [powder coated steel](/partitions/powder-coated-steel) can rust where the coating is damaged, so keep both for dry rooms. [Stainless steel](/partitions/stainless-steel) resists corrosion and suits premium public restrooms. Compare all five on the [partitions page](/partitions).

## The rules a Mesa restroom has to meet

- **Wheelchair access:** a restroom with stalls needs at least one stall a wheelchair user can enter and use. Arizona's disability law uses the federal sizes, and state law writes them into every local building code, so the city or county building department checks them.
- **Fire safety:** Mesa's building rules require solid plastic (HDPE) partitions to pass a fire test. Ask for the fire-rated version.
- **How many stalls:** Mesa sets the number of toilets by how many people use the building, and caps how many of the men's toilets may be urinals. The table below shows what that means.
- **Arizona and federal stall rules match:** Arizona adopts the federal standards for stalls, doors and grab bars, and state law says its rules may not go beyond them. A city may set stricter rules of its own.
- **Mesa access rules:** Mesa's building rules apply two accessibility standards, the federal one and a national standard in its 2017 edition, and the one that gives more access applies. A restroom meant mainly for children aged 3 through 12 may be designed to the federal guidelines for children instead.
- **Single-user and all-gender restrooms:** under Mesa's plumbing rules a one-person restroom must be marked as open to everyone. A shared all-gender room may take the place of separate men's and women's rooms if every toilet sits in its own compartment with a door and every urinal has its own partitioned area. A measure Arizona voters approved in November 2026 says that from January 1, 2027, a school or athletic association may not authorize anyone to use a restroom, locker room or shower room that is part of its athletics facilities and is not designated for that person's sex.
- **Permits:** Mesa issues tenant improvement permits for commercial interior work, with a faster on-site review for minor interior remodels. The city keeps its own list of work that needs no permit, and its commercial pages don't say whether replacing toilet partitions is on it. Ask Mesa Permit Services at 480-644-4273 whether your replacement needs one. You don't need a permit to buy partitions.

Rules change, so confirm your project with Mesa Development Services or your architect. The accessible stall's sizes are in our [ADA compliance guide](/guides/ada-compliance), and fixture spacing is in the [restroom layout guide](/guides/restroom-layout).

## How many stalls a Mesa restroom needs

Arizona has no statewide building rules, so Mesa's own rules decide. Mesa counts toilets by a building's capacity: the most people it is designed to hold at one time, worked out from its floor area. It isn't the number of staff or of visitors in a day. Half are counted as men and half as women. Every toilet needs a stall. Urinals are optional: in the men's room they may take the place of up to half of the toilets, or up to two thirds in restaurants and schools, so the table shows men's toilets as stalls.

:::stalls
head: Office | Restaurant | School
50: 1/1 | 1/1 | 1/1
100: 2/2 | 1/1 | 1/1
200: 3/3 | 2/2 | 2/2
400: 5/5 | 3/3 | 4/4
:::

These are minimums for the whole building, and at least one stall in each room must be wheelchair accessible. A shop designed for 100 people or fewer, and any other building or tenant space designed for 25 or fewer, doesn't need separate men's and women's rooms. Confirm the count with Mesa Development Services.

To find your building's capacity, Mesa's building rules count an office at one person per 150 square feet of floor, a restaurant dining room at one per 15 square feet and a classroom at one per 20. Your architect or Mesa Development Services sets the final number.

## What installation labor and sales tax add in Mesa

- **Local labor rates:** carpenters in the Phoenix–Mesa–Chandler area earn a median of $29.00 an hour, about the same as the national median of $29.12. It's a guide to local labor rates, not a quote: installers set their own prices.
- **Public jobs:** Arizona has no wage law for public jobs, and state law bars cities, counties and school districts from requiring prevailing wages in their contracts. On federally funded work the federal rate applies: $33.19 an hour plus $19.20 in benefits for a carpenter on building work in Maricopa County.
- **Sales tax:** 8.3% in Mesa: 5.6% state, 0.7% Maricopa County and 2% city.
- **Tax on installation:** in Arizona, a contractor's labor to repair, replace or alter part of an existing building is not taxed. The contractor pays tax on the materials and adds no tax line to the invoice. New construction, and alterations to a commercial building under a contract above $750,000, are taxed as contracting on 65% of the contract price.
- **Buying without installation:** partitions shipped to Mesa are taxable. A delivery charge listed separately on the invoice is not. An out-of-state seller with more than $100,000 a year in Arizona sales collects the tax. If the seller doesn't collect it, the buyer owes use tax to the state.

How tax applies depends on your contract, so confirm with your accountant or the Arizona Department of Revenue.
