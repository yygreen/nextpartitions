# Ceiling Hung vs Floor Mounted vs Floor-to-Ceiling: Choosing the Right Mounting Style

Ceiling hung vs floor mounted partitions is really a question about your building. The mounting style decides what holds the stalls up, what the floor and ceiling have to carry, how the restroom cleans and how well the stalls stand up to rough use. The door and panel material is a separate choice you make after.

There's also a fourth style the title leaves out: overhead braced. The pilasters (the posts at the front of each stall) are anchored to the floor, and a headrail across their tops ties them to the walls. Manufacturers describe it as economical and sturdy, and it installs almost anywhere, so it's the usual default. This partition mounting styles comparison covers all four, side by side, using what manufacturers and public specifications publish.

## What holds each style up

### Overhead braced
Each pilaster is anchored to the floor on a leveling bracket hidden by a stainless steel shoe. A continuous anodized aluminum headrail runs across the pilaster tops and fastens to the walls, which keeps the stalls rigid from top to bottom. Its anti-grip profile is hard to grab or hang from. [Overhead braced](/mounting-styles/overhead-braced) is also called headrail braced or floor anchored/overhead braced.

![Overhead braced toilet partitions, front view](/images/diagrams/mounting-overhead-braced-front.svg)

1. **Headrail:** extruded aluminum, anti-grip
2. **Pilaster shoe:** 4" stainless steel
3. **Pilaster:** 82" here, some lines 83"

### Floor mounted
[Floor mounted](/mounting-styles/floor-mounted) partitions, also called floor anchored, stand on their own. Each pilaster is bolted into the floor and there's no headrail, so nothing runs above the doors. The anchors do all the work. Specs set the anchors at least 2" into structural concrete, typically at least 3" thick, and a pilaster at least 8" wide is recommended.

![Floor mounted toilet partitions, front view](/images/diagrams/mounting-floor-mounted-front.svg)

1. **Floor anchors:** structural concrete
2. **Pilaster:** 69" floor-anchored stile, no headrail

### Ceiling hung
[Ceiling hung](/mounting-styles/ceiling-hung) partitions, also called ceiling mounted or suspended, hang from structural steel above the finished ceiling and never touch the floor. A threaded rod runs from the steel through the ceiling into each pilaster. The key rule: the ceiling tile or drywall carries nothing. All the load goes to the steel above it.

![Ceiling hung toilet partitions, front view](/images/diagrams/mounting-ceiling-hung-front.svg)

1. **Structural steel above the ceiling:** by others
2. **Hanger:** from the steel into each pilaster
3. **Clear floor:** nothing touches it

### Floor to ceiling
[Floor to ceiling](/mounting-styles/floor-to-ceiling) partitions anchor each pilaster at both ends: into the concrete floor and into structural support at the ceiling. Because the stalls are secured to the floor, ceiling and walls, manufacturers describe them as very stable, strong and resistant to abuse.

![Floor to ceiling toilet partitions, front view](/images/diagrams/mounting-floor-to-ceiling-front.svg)

1. **Floor anchor:** at each pilaster foot
2. **Ceiling anchor:** into structure at the top
3. **Doors and panels:** 58" at 12" above the floor

## What your building has to provide

**The floor.** Overhead braced goes in on most floors that accept anchors. A typical spec calls for anchors at least 1-3/4" into the structural floor for overhead-braced units, against 2" for floor-anchored units. Floor mounted is for concrete floors only, because without a headrail each pilaster depends entirely on its anchors. Floor to ceiling needs a concrete floor at every pilaster too. Ceiling hung is the only style with nothing in the floor. For any floor-anchored style, ask your contractor or structural engineer to confirm that anchors can go in at each pilaster location, especially over in-slab services.

**The ceiling.** Overhead braced and floor mounted need nothing from the ceiling. Manufacturers recommend these two when adequate ceiling support can't be provided. The other two need structure overhead, and none of it comes with the partitions:

- **Ceiling hung** needs a steel member above the finished ceiling at every pilaster, such as an I-beam, channel or angle. The structure has to carry roughly 275 to 295 lb per partition, depending on the material, and stay rigid when doors swing and hit the pilasters. The architect or structural engineer designs the mounting system, including any lateral bracing. Wood framing isn't suitable.
- **Floor to ceiling** needs structural members in the ceiling, supplied by other trades. Because the floor carries part of the work, wood may give adequate overhead support here. Check with the manufacturer.

**The walls.** In every style, panels attach to the walls with brackets, so confirm the wall and floor backing before installation.

## Heights and floor clearance

The mounting style changes how tall the pilasters are, not how tall the doors and panels are. In every style, standard doors and panels are 58" high, mounted 12" above the finished floor (see our [stall dimensions guide](/guides/bathroom-stall-dimensions)). Solid plastic lines often run 55" mounted 14" above the floor.

What changes is the pilaster (see the table above). Floor mounted pilasters are the shortest, at around 69", because there's no headrail to reach. Ceiling hung and floor to ceiling pilasters are made to the room, for ceilings up to about 10'. For taller rooms, ask before you specify.

"Floor to ceiling" describes the pilasters, not the doors and panels. In standard lines the doors and panels have the usual gap underneath. Taller doors that sit closer to the floor are a separate privacy option (see below).

If you lower the gap for privacy, check toe clearance in the wheelchair accessible stall. Under 2010 ADA Standards section 604.8.1.4, the front partition and at least one side partition need 9" of toe clearance above the finished floor and 6" deep (12" high in children's compartments). Exceptions apply to larger compartments: no front toe clearance when the compartment is over 62" deep with a wall-hung toilet or over 65" deep with a floor-mounted one, and no side toe clearance when it's over 66" wide. See our [ADA compliance guide](/guides/ada-compliance).

## Materials by mounting style

Not every material comes in every style. This is the most common reason a style gets ruled out late.

| [Powder coated steel](/partitions/powder-coated-steel) | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| [Stainless steel](/partitions/stainless-steel) | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| [Plastic laminate](/partitions/plastic-laminate) | Yes | Yes | Yes | Not in every line |
| [Phenolic](/partitions/phenolic) | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes, including gap-free versions |
| [Solid plastic (HDPE)](/partitions/solid-plastic-hdpe) | Yes | Generally not recommended | A few lines only | Yes |

Across the manufacturers we carry; any single product line may offer fewer styles. Solid plastic is the one to watch. Several manufacturers don't recommend floor-anchored or ceiling-hung mounting for solid-plastic units. Some HDPE lines are offered overhead braced, ceiling hung or floor to ceiling, but not floor anchored alone. The few solid-plastic lines that come ceiling hung may need exposed cross bracing.

## Cleaning, strength and privacy

**Cleaning.** Ceiling hung wins. With no pilaster shoes on the floor, and wall-hung fixtures, the entire floor is open for cleaning. In the other three styles, the shoes stay on the floor.

**Strength and abuse.** Floor to ceiling is the most rigid, with every pilaster anchored at both ends and the panels fastened to the walls. Manufacturers recommend it for high-traffic areas such as schools and other spaces that need extra security. Overhead braced is stiff at the top as well as the bottom, because the headrail ties the pilasters together. For the toughest restrooms, pair the style with a durable material such as [phenolic](/partitions/phenolic), [solid plastic (HDPE)](/partitions/solid-plastic-hdpe) or [stainless steel](/partitions/stainless-steel).

**Nothing to grab overhead.** Floor mounted and floor to ceiling have no rail across the stall tops. Overhead braced does. Its headrail is anti-grip, but specs for behavioral health units rule out overhead connecting framing that could be used as an anchor point for hanging, and the headrail is that kind of framing.

**Privacy.** No mounting style closes the gaps on its own, floor to ceiling included. Privacy comes from the doors and panels: taller doors set closer to the floor, and no-sightline or gap-free edges. Some lines offer 64" or 72" doors and panels at 6" or 9" above the floor in overhead braced. Some floor mounted lines offer 64" at 6" or 9". Floor to ceiling lines go up to 72", and some to 92" at 4", 6" or 9". If privacy is the goal, say so in your quote request.

## Coordination and cost

Manufacturers describe overhead braced as economical. Beyond that, we don't compare prices by style here; ask us to quote the options side by side. What clearly differs is the coordination each style needs:

- **Overhead braced:** the least. Floor anchors and wall backing, no ceiling work.
- **Floor mounted:** a sound concrete slab at every pilaster. No ceiling work.
- **Floor to ceiling:** two sets of anchors. Ceiling members come from other trades, and pilasters are cut to the measured ceiling height, so the ceiling must be finished first.
- **Ceiling hung:** the most of any style. The structural steel, its design and its installation come from other trades, usually under the metal fabrications section of the specs. Bring it into the drawings early.

## How to choose: a decision guide

Answer these in order. The first "yes" usually decides it.

1. **Is this a behavioral health or patient-safety area?** Rule out overhead braced and ask your design team about ligature-resistant options.
2. **Can the floor not take anchors, or must it stay completely clear for wet cleaning?** Ceiling hung, if engineered steel can go above the ceiling. If it can't, you need a floor-anchored style.
3. **Heavy traffic or abuse, as in schools and public restrooms?** Floor to ceiling, if the floor is concrete and the ceiling has structural members at each pilaster.
4. **High ceiling, or no headrail wanted for the look?** Floor mounted, on structural concrete. If you want solid plastic, choose overhead braced or floor to ceiling instead.
5. **None of the above, or no structure above the ceiling?** Overhead braced. It needs no ceiling structure, fits most floors and comes in every material we sell.

Then check the material table and the ceiling height.

## Switching styles on an existing restroom

Sometimes. It depends on what the building already has.

- **To ceiling hung:** you need steel at every pilaster above the finished ceiling, designed for the load. Wood framing won't do. The style suits modernization projects where a drop ceiling or open span construction can be used.
- **To floor to ceiling:** you need structural members at the ceiling (wood may be adequate) and a concrete floor. The pilasters are made to the measured ceiling height.
- **To floor mounted:** you need structural concrete of adequate thickness at every pilaster, with no in-slab services in the way.
- **To overhead braced:** it asks the least of the building and is the fallback when the ceiling can't carry a load.

Pilaster heights differ by style (around 69" floor mounted, 82" to 83" overhead braced), so plan on pilasters made for the new style. Have your contractor or engineer confirm the structure first. Our [installation guide](/guides/how-to-install-toilet-partitions) explains what happens on site.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between ceiling hung and floor mounted partitions?
Ceiling hung partitions hang from structural steel above the ceiling and never touch the floor. Floor mounted partitions are bolted into a structural concrete floor with no headrail and nothing overhead. Ceiling hung keeps the floor clear for cleaning but needs engineered steel; floor mounted needs no ceiling work but needs a sound slab.

### What is the most common toilet partition mounting style?
Overhead braced: floor-anchored pilasters tied together by a headrail. Manufacturers describe it as economical and sturdy and say it installs almost anywhere. It needs no ceiling structure and comes in all five common materials.

### Do floor to ceiling bathroom stalls go all the way to the floor?
Not in standard lines. The pilasters run floor to ceiling, but the doors and panels are standard height with a gap underneath. Taller doors with smaller gaps are a separate privacy option.

### Which mounting style is strongest?
Floor to ceiling. Every pilaster is anchored at the floor and the ceiling, and manufacturers recommend it for high-traffic spaces such as schools.

### Can ceiling hung partitions hang from a drop ceiling?
Not from the grid or tiles. The pilasters hang on rods that pass through the ceiling to steel above it. The finished ceiling carries no load.

### Are headrail braced and overhead braced the same thing?
Yes. Headrail braced, overhead braced and floor anchored/overhead braced all describe floor-anchored pilasters with a continuous headrail across the top.
