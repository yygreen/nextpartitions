## The right material for Chicago's climate

Chicago gets about 38 inches of rain a year, on roughly 126 days, and relative humidity averages 80% in the morning and 60% in the afternoon. What that means for partitions depends on whether the restroom is heated and ventilated:

- **Heated, ventilated restrooms** (offices, restaurants, clinics, most schools): all five materials work. Choose on looks, cleaning routine and budget.
- **Unheated restrooms or rooms open to outside air** (parks, stadiums, warehouses, pool and locker rooms): choose [solid plastic (HDPE)](/partitions/solid-plastic-hdpe) or [phenolic](/partitions/phenolic). Both are solid all the way through and handle constant moisture.

[Plastic laminate](/partitions/plastic-laminate) has a particleboard core that may swell with excessive moisture, and [powder coated steel](/partitions/powder-coated-steel) can rust where the coating is damaged, so keep both for dry rooms. [Stainless steel](/partitions/stainless-steel) resists corrosion and suits premium public restrooms. Compare all five on the [partitions page](/partitions).

## The rules a Chicago restroom has to meet

- **Wheelchair access:** a restroom with stalls needs at least one stall a wheelchair user can enter and use, and a room with six or more toilets and urinals also needs a narrower stall with grab bars on both sides. Federal law and Illinois's own access rules set the sizes, and the state rules can't be waived. On work costing $50,000 or more, an Illinois architect or engineer must state in writing that the plans comply, and the office that issues the building permit checks them.
- **Fire safety:** Chicago has its own building rules. They treat toilet partitions as an interior finish and require solid plastic (HDPE) and polypropylene partitions to pass a room-corner fire test. Ask for the fire-rated version.
- **How many stalls:** Chicago has its own plumbing rules, separate from the state's. They set the number of toilets by how many people use the building, and every toilet used by staff or the public needs a stall with a door. The table further down shows what that means.
- **Illinois and federal stall rules match:** Illinois's access rules for stall sizes, doors, toe space and grab bars read the same as the federal ones, so a stall that meets one meets the other. Illinois adds that the floor around the toilet must be level.
- **Single-user and all-gender restrooms:** in Illinois every one-person restroom in a public building or a place open to the public must be marked as a restroom for all genders, with no gender on the sign. Chicago adds a lock that shows from outside when the room is in use and a covered waste bin. A shared restroom may be made all-gender. In Chicago each toilet then sits in its own compartment with solid walls and one door, with no gaps between 4.5 inches and 7 feet above the floor, walls that reach 9 feet or to within 2 inches of the ceiling, a lock that shows when it is in use and a covered waste bin inside. Illinois law also requires dividers from floor to ceiling, a privacy strip at the door and no urinals, and in a newly built or converted room a menstrual supply dispenser and a baby changing station.
- **Permits:** Chicago requires a building permit for most construction, alteration and repair work. Its list of work that needs none covers painting, tiling, cabinets, countertops and similar finish work, and movable partitions no taller than 7 feet that aren't fixed in place. It doesn't name fixed toilet partitions, so ask the Chicago Department of Buildings (312-744-3449) whether your replacement needs one. You don't need a permit to buy partitions.

Rules change, so confirm your project with the Chicago Department of Buildings or your architect. The accessible stall's sizes are in our [ADA compliance guide](/guides/ada-compliance), and fixture spacing is in the [restroom layout guide](/guides/restroom-layout).

## How many stalls a Chicago restroom needs

Chicago counts toilets by a building's capacity: the most people it is designed to hold at one time, worked out from its floor area. Half are counted as men and half as women, and any part of a group counts as a whole one. Every toilet needs a stall with a door. The counts apply to new buildings, additions and changes of use. Urinals are optional. In restaurants and schools they may take the place of up to two thirds of the men's toilets, and in offices up to half. Each urinal needs a screen on both sides, at least 30 inches apart and reaching at least 5 feet above the floor.

:::stalls
head: Office | Restaurant | School
50: 2/2 | 1/1 | 2/2
100: 3/3 | 2/2 | 3/3
200: 5/5 | 4/4 | 5/5
400: 9/9 | 8/6 | 9/9
:::

These are minimums for the whole building. For offices and schools the city's table gives a single figure, not one for men and one for women. The numbers here apply it to each half of the building's capacity. Applied to the whole building at once it gives one toilet fewer, so confirm the count with the Chicago Department of Buildings. A space for 30 people or fewer may have one single-user restroom. Stores under 7,500 square feet and takeout counters with no more than 400 square feet of public area need no public restroom. At least one stall in each room must be wheelchair accessible.

To find your building's capacity, Chicago's building rules count an office at one person per 100 square feet of floor, a restaurant dining room at one per 15 square feet and a classroom at one per 20. The plumbing rules allow a lower number where not everyone is in the building at the same time. Your architect or the Chicago Department of Buildings sets the final number.
