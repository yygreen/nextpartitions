## The right material for Atlanta's climate

Atlanta gets about 50 inches of rain a year, on roughly 115 days, and relative humidity averages 81% in the morning and 55% in the afternoon. In most Atlanta restrooms the room decides the material, not the weather:

- **Air-conditioned restrooms** (offices, restaurants, clinics, most schools): all five materials work. Choose on looks, cleaning routine and budget.
- **Restrooms without air conditioning, or that get wet from the inside** (warehouses, plants, parks, stadiums, pool and locker rooms): choose [solid plastic (HDPE)](/partitions/solid-plastic-hdpe) or [phenolic](/partitions/phenolic). Both are solid all the way through and handle constant moisture.

[Plastic laminate](/partitions/plastic-laminate) has a particleboard core that may swell with excessive moisture, and [powder coated steel](/partitions/powder-coated-steel) can rust where the coating is damaged, so keep both for dry rooms. [Stainless steel](/partitions/stainless-steel) resists corrosion and suits premium public restrooms. Compare all five on the [partitions page](/partitions).

## The rules an Atlanta restroom has to meet

- **Wheelchair access:** a restroom with stalls needs at least one stall a wheelchair user can enter and use. Georgia's own access rules adopt the federal standards, and the State Fire Marshal or the local building department enforces them. A stall that is replaced, not just repaired, must meet the current rules.
- **Fire safety:** Georgia's building rules require solid plastic (HDPE) partitions to pass a room fire test. Ask for the fire-rated version. In new buildings the State Fire Marshal's rules govern wall finishes, so confirm with your local fire marshal.
- **How many stalls:** Georgia's plumbing rules set the number of toilets by how many people use the building, and Atlanta enforces them. The table below shows what that means.
- **Georgia and federal stall rules match:** Georgia uses the federal standards for stalls, doors and grab bars as written, so a stall that meets one meets the other.
- **Single-user and all-gender restrooms:** a one-person restroom must be marked as open to everyone. Georgia's rules call for separate men's and women's rooms for the toilets a building must have, and the state removed the option that lets a shared all-gender room take their place, so ask your building department before planning one. State law requires public schools, and private schools that compete against them, to provide separate shared restrooms for each sex.
- **Permits:** Atlanta requires a permit for new construction and for specific alterations to existing buildings, including structural, plumbing, mechanical and electrical changes. The city's permit guidance doesn't name toilet partitions, so ask the Atlanta Office of Buildings whether your replacement needs one. Work at the airport needs the Department of Aviation's approval before plans go to the Office of Buildings. You don't need a permit to buy partitions.

Rules change, so confirm your project with the Atlanta Office of Buildings or your architect. The accessible stall's sizes are in our [ADA compliance guide](/guides/ada-compliance), and fixture spacing is in the [restroom layout guide](/guides/restroom-layout).

## How many stalls an Atlanta restroom needs

Georgia's rules count toilets by a building's capacity: the most people it is designed to hold at one time, worked out from its floor area. Half are counted as men and half as women, and any fraction rounds up. Every toilet needs a stall. Urinals are optional: in the men's room they may take the place of up to half the toilets in an office, and up to two thirds in a restaurant or school, and each urinal needs a screen on both sides.

:::stalls
head: Office | Restaurant | School
50: 1/1 | 1/1 | 1/1
100: 2/2 | 1/1 | 1/1
200: 3/3 | 2/2 | 2/2
400: 5/5 | 3/3 | 4/4
:::

These are state minimums for the whole building. A city may set stricter ones, so confirm the count with the Atlanta Office of Buildings. An office that holds 25 people or fewer, or any space that holds 15 or fewer, may use one restroom for everyone. At least one stall in each room must be wheelchair accessible.

To find your building's capacity, the building rules count an office at one person per 150 square feet of floor, a restaurant dining room at one per 15 square feet and a classroom at one per 20. Your architect or the Atlanta Office of Buildings sets the final number.

## What installation labor and sales tax add in Atlanta

- **Local labor rates:** carpenters in the Atlanta–Sandy Springs–Roswell area earn a median of $24.37 an hour, about 16% below the national median of $29.12. It's a guide to local labor rates, not a quote: installers set their own prices.
- **Public jobs:** Georgia has no state wage law for public construction, so a set wage applies only on federally funded projects, under federal law. For a carpenter on building work in Fulton County that rate is $17.48 an hour, with no benefits amount listed.
- **Sales tax:** 8.9% in Atlanta: 4% state, 1% local option, 1% schools, 1.5% MARTA, 1% city and 0.4% city transportation tax.
- **Tax on installation:** in Georgia, installation labor is not taxed when it is listed separately on the invoice. A contractor who supplies and installs the partitions pays the tax on the materials.
- **Buying without installation:** partitions shipped to Atlanta are taxable, freight included. If the seller doesn't collect the tax, the buyer owes it to the state.

How tax applies depends on your contract, so confirm with your accountant or the Georgia Department of Revenue.
