## The right material for Albuquerque's climate

Albuquerque gets about 9 inches of rain a year, on roughly 59 days, and relative humidity averages 53% in the morning and 29% in the afternoon. On average the wettest month is July (about 1.6 inches) and the driest is January (about 0.4 inches). The weather rarely decides the material here. The room does:

- **Most restrooms** (offices, restaurants, retail, clinics, schools): all five materials work. Choose on looks, cleaning routine and budget.
- **Rooms that get wet from the inside** (pool facilities, gym locker rooms, restrooms that are hosed down): choose [solid plastic (HDPE)](/partitions/solid-plastic-hdpe) or [phenolic](/partitions/phenolic). Both are solid all the way through and handle constant moisture.

Keep [plastic laminate](/partitions/plastic-laminate) and [powder coated steel](/partitions/powder-coated-steel) for dry rooms; [stainless steel](/partitions/stainless-steel) resists corrosion. Compare all five on the [partitions page](/partitions).

## The rules an Albuquerque restroom has to meet

- **Wheelchair access:** a restroom with stalls needs at least one stall a wheelchair user can enter and use. Federal law and New Mexico's own building rules set the sizes, and the building department checks them when it reviews the plans.
- **Fire safety:** New Mexico's building rules require solid plastic (HDPE) partitions to pass a fire test. Ask for the fire-rated version.
- **How many stalls:** Albuquerque applies New Mexico's building rules, which set the number of toilets by how many people use the building. The table below shows what that means.
- **New Mexico extras:** stalls here need a second, upright grab bar on the side wall, and a wheelchair stall needs more toe room under its partitions (12 inches high, against 9 under the federal rules). A restroom with 20 or more toilets needs two accessible ones. Where children are the main users, as in a school or day care, the stalls must be built to children's sizes. A new public restroom needs a baby changing station on each floor that has one.
- **Single-user and all-gender restrooms:** state law requires a one-person restroom in a place open to the public to be available to anyone and marked with a gender-neutral sign. A shared all-gender room is allowed: the toilets are then counted for everyone in the building together, and every urinal must be in a stall.
- **Permits:** Albuquerque requires a permit to alter or repair a building and lists the work that needs none. The list covers painting, papering, tiling, carpeting, cabinets, countertops and similar finish work, movable partitions no taller than 5 feet 9 inches, and repairs worth less than $1,000. It doesn't name toilet partitions fixed to the floor or wall, so ask the city's Building Safety Division (505-924-3320) whether your replacement needs one. You don't need a permit to buy partitions.

Rules change, so confirm your project with Albuquerque's Building Safety Division or your architect. The accessible stall's sizes are in our [ADA compliance guide](/guides/ada-compliance), and fixture spacing is in the [restroom layout guide](/guides/restroom-layout).

## How many stalls an Albuquerque restroom needs

Albuquerque uses New Mexico's rules without changing the counts. They count toilets by a building's capacity: the most people it is designed to hold at one time, worked out from its floor area. It isn't the number of staff or of visitors in a day. Half are counted as men and half as women. Every toilet needs a stall. Urinals are optional: they may replace up to half of the men's toilets in an office and up to two thirds in a restaurant or school, so you can buy fewer men's stalls and add urinal screens.

:::stalls
head: Office | Restaurant | School
50: 1/1 | 1/1 | 1/1
100: 2/2 | 1/1 | 1/1
200: 3/3 | 2/2 | 2/2
400: 5/5 | 3/3 | 4/4
:::

These are minimums for the whole building, and at least one stall in each room must be wheelchair accessible. New Mexico's rules don't say clearly whether a small business may have one shared restroom, so ask Albuquerque's Building Safety Division.

To find your building's capacity, the building rules count an office at one person per 150 square feet of floor, a restaurant dining room at one per 15 square feet and a classroom at one per 20. Your architect or Albuquerque's Building Safety Division sets the final number.

## What installation labor and sales tax add in Albuquerque

- **Local labor rates:** carpenters in the Albuquerque area earn a median of $28.89 an hour, about the same as the national median of $29.12. It's a guide to local labor rates, not a quote: installers set their own prices.
- **Public jobs:** on state, city, county and school projects over $60,000, New Mexico law requires at least the wage and benefits the state sets each year for each trade. For a carpenter on building work in Bernalillo County the 2026 rate is $32.12 an hour plus $14.28 in benefits, and the employer also pays $0.60 an hour into an apprenticeship fund.
- **Gross receipts tax:** 7.625% in Albuquerque: the 4.875% state rate plus 2.75% in local taxes. Rates normally change only on July 1.
- **Tax on installation:** New Mexico has a gross receipts tax in place of a sales tax, and it covers services. Installation labor is taxed, even when it's billed separately, at the rate of the job site. On a supply-and-install contract the contractor's whole price is taxed.
- **Buying without installation:** partitions shipped to Albuquerque are taxable, including freight billed by the seller. If the seller doesn't charge the tax, the buyer owes the matching compensating tax to the state.

How tax applies depends on your contract, so confirm with your accountant or the New Mexico Taxation and Revenue Department.
