## The right material for Omaha's climate

Omaha gets about 32 inches of rain a year, on roughly 100 days, and relative humidity averages 81% in the morning and 59% in the afternoon. On average the wettest month is May (about 4.7 inches) and the driest is January (about 0.8 inches). What that means for partitions depends on whether the restroom is heated and ventilated:

- **Heated, ventilated restrooms** (offices, restaurants, clinics, most schools): all five materials work. Choose on looks, cleaning routine and budget.
- **Unheated restrooms or rooms open to outside air** (parks, stadiums, warehouses, pool and locker rooms): choose [solid plastic (HDPE)](/partitions/solid-plastic-hdpe) or [phenolic](/partitions/phenolic). Both are solid all the way through and handle constant moisture.

Keep [plastic laminate](/partitions/plastic-laminate) and [powder coated steel](/partitions/powder-coated-steel) for dry rooms; [stainless steel](/partitions/stainless-steel) resists corrosion. Compare all five on the [partitions page](/partitions).

## The rules an Omaha restroom has to meet

- **Wheelchair access:** a restroom with stalls needs at least one stall a wheelchair user can enter and use, and a room with six or more toilets and urinals also needs a narrower stall with grab bars on both sides. Federal law sets the sizes, and Nebraska's own access rules adopt the federal ones. In Omaha the city checks plans for commercial work against them, and a building can't get its occupancy permit until it complies.
- **Fire safety:** Omaha's building rules require solid plastic (HDPE) and polypropylene partitions to pass a room-corner fire test. Ask for the fire-rated version.
- **How many stalls:** Omaha has its own plumbing rules, separate from Nebraska's, and they set the number of toilets and urinals by the kind of building and how many people use it. The table below shows what that means.
- **Nebraska extras:** the state's building rules add a national accessibility standard on top of the federal one. It asks for an 18 inch vertical grab bar on the side wall next to the toilet, which the federal rules do not. In a very large restroom, 5% of the stalls must be wheelchair accessible, which means a second one from 21 stalls up.
- **Stall size:** in Omaha every toilet used by the public or by employees needs its own stall with a door, at least 30 inches wide and 60 inches long. A partition is also required between a urinal and a sink.
- **Single-user and all-gender restrooms:** Omaha's rules call for separate men's and women's restrooms, each marked for one sex, and say no person may use a restroom assigned to the other. A "unisex restroom" under Omaha's rules is a lockable one-person room with a toilet, a sink and a urinal, a "Unisex Restroom" sign or symbol and a sign showing whether it is occupied. One such room can serve a business of under 2,000 square feet with four or fewer employees. Unisex rooms aren't allowed at swimming pools, in malls, in places that serve food or drink or, with narrow exceptions, in schools. Places of assembly and stores that need six or more toilets and urinals must add a family restroom. The rules don't provide for a shared all-gender room with stalls, so ask Omaha Permits and Inspections before planning one.
- **Permits:** ask Omaha Permits and Inspections (402-444-5350) whether replacing or adding partitions in an existing restroom needs a building permit. You don't need a permit to buy partitions.

Rules change, so confirm your project with Omaha Permits and Inspections or your architect. The accessible stall's sizes are in our [ADA compliance guide](/guides/ada-compliance), and fixture spacing is in the [restroom layout guide](/guides/restroom-layout).

## How many stalls an Omaha restroom needs

Omaha counts toilets by a building's capacity: the most people it is designed to hold at one time, worked out from its floor area. Half are counted as men and half as women, and each half is read against the city's table for that kind of building. Every toilet needs a stall. In offices and larger restaurants the men's room needs urinals as well as toilets, and the women's room needs more toilets than the men's.

:::stalls
head: Office | Restaurant | Elementary school
50: 3/1+1 | 1/1 | 1/~1
100: 4/1+1 | 2/1 | 2/~2
200: 6/3+2 | 4/1+1 | 4/~4
400: 8/5+3 | 5/2+1 | 8/~8
:::

The restaurant column also covers restaurants that serve alcohol with meals. Bars and other places serving liquor follow a stricter table, and outdoor seating counts once it passes a quarter of the indoor seats. The school column counts students only, at one toilet for every 25 elementary students. Secondary schools need one for every 30, and staff toilets are separate and extra. Omaha's school table doesn't say how many of the boys' fixtures may be urinals, so confirm the split with Omaha Permits and Inspections. In a building with several floors, Omaha counts each floor on its own. At least one stall in each room must be wheelchair accessible.

To find your building's capacity, Omaha's plumbing rules count a professional office at one person per 160 square feet, leaving out break rooms, lobbies and other shared areas. A restaurant is counted at one person per 15 square feet after taking out the kitchen, bar, restrooms and entries, and a school at one student per 35 square feet. Your architect or Omaha Permits and Inspections sets the final number.

## What installation labor and sales tax add in Omaha

- **Local labor rates:** carpenters in the Omaha area earn a median of $25.39 an hour, about 13% below the national median of $29.12. It's a guide to local labor rates, not a quote: installers set their own prices.
- **Public jobs:** Nebraska does not set wage rates for public jobs: state law has contractors bidding on public works confirm that they pay what at least half of the contractors in their trade pay. Where federal money is involved, the federal rate applies: $33.42 an hour plus $18.66 in benefits for a carpenter on building work in Douglas County.
- **Sales tax:** 7% in Omaha: 5.5% state and 1.5% city.
- **Tax on installation:** in Nebraska it depends on how the installer is registered with the state. Some contractors charge sales tax on the partitions and none on labor shown as a separate line; if the two aren't separated, the whole invoice is taxed. Others pay the tax when they buy the partitions and add none to the job. The state's guidance doesn't name toilet partitions, or cover an installer fitting partitions you bought yourself, so ask.
- **Buying without installation:** partitions shipped to Omaha are taxable, and so is freight charged by the seller. If the seller doesn't collect the tax, the buyer owes it to the state.

How tax applies depends on your contract, so confirm with your accountant or the Nebraska Department of Revenue.

## FAQ

### Do I need a permit to replace toilet partitions in Omaha?
Ask Omaha Permits and Inspections (402-444-5350) whether replacing or adding partitions in an existing restroom needs a building permit. You don't need a permit to buy partitions.

### How many stalls does an Omaha restroom need?
For a building that holds 100 people, an office needs at least 4 women's stalls, 1 men's stall and 1 urinal, a restaurant needs at least 2 women's and 1 men's stall and an elementary school needs at least 2 women's stalls and 2 men's stalls or urinals. The table above covers other sizes. Confirm the number for your building with Omaha Permits and Inspections.

### Who can install toilet partitions in Omaha?
A contractor you hire. We supply the partitions and the shop drawings. In Nebraska that contractor registers with the state Department of Labor, and you check Omaha's own licensing rule with Omaha Permits and Inspections. ### Which partition material is best for Omaha's climate?
In a heated, ventilated restroom, any of the five. In an unheated restroom or one open to outside air, choose solid plastic (HDPE) or phenolic, which handle constant moisture.

### What tax applies to partitions delivered to Omaha?
7% in Omaha: 5.5% state and 1.5% city. How tax applies to installation depends on your contract, so confirm with your accountant or the Nebraska Department of Revenue.
