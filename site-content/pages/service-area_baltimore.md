## The right material for Baltimore's climate

Baltimore gets about 45 inches of rain a year, on roughly 117 days, and relative humidity averages 77% in the morning and 53% in the afternoon. On average the wettest month is July (about 4.5 inches) and the driest is February (about 2.9 inches). In most Baltimore restrooms the room decides the material, not the weather:

- **Air-conditioned restrooms** (offices, restaurants, clinics, most schools): all five materials work. Choose on looks, cleaning routine and budget.
- **Restrooms without air conditioning, or that get wet from the inside** (warehouses, plants, parks, stadiums, pool and locker rooms): choose [solid plastic (HDPE)](/partitions/solid-plastic-hdpe) or [phenolic](/partitions/phenolic). Both are solid all the way through and handle constant moisture.

Keep [plastic laminate](/partitions/plastic-laminate) and [powder coated steel](/partitions/powder-coated-steel) for dry rooms; [stainless steel](/partitions/stainless-steel) resists corrosion. Compare all five on the [partitions page](/partitions).

## The rules a Baltimore restroom has to meet

- **Wheelchair access:** a restroom with stalls needs at least one stall a wheelchair user can enter and use. Federal law and Maryland's own access rules set the sizes. There is no separate state review: your local building department checks access when it reviews the plans.
- **Fire safety:** Baltimore's building rules require solid plastic (HDPE) partitions to pass a room-corner fire test. Ask for the fire-rated version.
- **How many stalls:** Baltimore's building rules set the number of toilets by how many people use the building. The table below shows what that means.
- **Maryland and federal stall rules match:** Maryland's access rules adopt the federal ones and give the same size for a wheelchair stall: at least 60 inches wide, and 56 inches deep with a wall-hung toilet or 59 inches with a floor-mounted one.
- **Single-user and all-gender restrooms:** a Baltimore ordinance requires every one-person public restroom in a business, a place open to the public or a city building to be open to any gender and to carry a sign that names no gender, such as "Restroom", or a symbol. The city gives a written warning first and can issue a citation if the restroom isn't corrected within 30 days. Baltimore's building rules allow a shared all-gender room in place of separate men's and women's rooms if every toilet sits in its own compartment with a door and the urinals are in a visually separate area or each in a stall. Maryland has no state law limiting who may use which restroom, and its public accommodations law bars turning someone away because of gender identity.
- **Permits:** Baltimore requires a permit to alter or repair a building and publishes a list of work that needs none. The list covers interior painting, papering, tiling, carpeting, cabinets, countertops and similar interior finish work, and movable partitions no taller than 5 feet 9 inches. It doesn't name toilet partitions fixed to the floor or wall, so ask the Baltimore City permits office (443-984-1809) whether your replacement needs one. You don't need a permit to buy partitions.

Rules change, so confirm your project with the Baltimore City permits office at the Department of Housing and Community Development or your architect. The accessible stall's sizes are in our [ADA compliance guide](/guides/ada-compliance), and fixture spacing is in the [restroom layout guide](/guides/restroom-layout).

## How many stalls a Baltimore restroom needs

Baltimore's building rules count toilets by a building's capacity: the most people it is designed to hold at one time, worked out from its floor area. It isn't the number of staff or of visitors in a day. Half are counted as men and half as women. Every toilet needs a stall, and every urinal needs a screen on each side. Urinals are optional: in a men's room they may replace up to half of the toilets in an office and up to two thirds in a restaurant or school, so the table shows the men's side as stalls.

:::stalls
head: Office | Restaurant | School
50: 1/1 | 1/1 | 1/1
100: 2/2 | 1/1 | 1/1
200: 3/3 | 2/2 | 2/2
400: 5/5 | 3/3 | 4/4
:::

These are Baltimore's minimums for the whole building, and at least one stall in each room must be wheelchair accessible. A men's room that needs only one toilet can't swap it for a urinal. An office designed for 25 people or fewer, or any space for 15 or fewer, may have one shared restroom instead of separate men's and women's rooms. A shared all-gender room is counted on everyone in the building instead of half. A restaurant's seasonal outdoor seating counts toward the number of people.

To find your building's capacity, Baltimore's building rules count an office at one person per 150 square feet of floor, a restaurant dining room at one per 15 square feet and a classroom at one per 20.

## What installation labor and sales tax add in Baltimore

- **Local labor rates:** carpenters in the Baltimore–Columbia–Towson area earn a median of $29.99 an hour, about 3% above the national median of $29.12. It's a guide to local labor rates, not a quote: installers set their own prices.
- **Public jobs:** on public projects of $250,000 or more that are run by the State, or where the State pays at least a quarter of the cost, school construction included, Maryland law requires at least the prevailing wage set by the state's Commissioner of Labor and Industry for each county and for Baltimore City. For a carpenter on building work in Baltimore City that is $35.89 an hour plus $14.60 in benefits. Baltimore has its own wage rule for city construction contracts over $5,000: the city's 2026 schedule sets the same $35.89 an hour plus $14.60 in benefits for a carpenter on building work.
- **Sales tax:** 6% in Baltimore. That is Maryland's state rate, and state law doesn't let a city or county add a general sales tax of its own.
- **Tax on installation:** in Maryland, installation labor is not taxed when it is listed separately on the invoice. A contractor who supplies and installs materials that become part of the building pays the tax when buying those materials and does not add sales tax to the contract.
- **Buying without installation:** partitions shipped to Baltimore are taxable. Freight is not taxed when it is listed separately on the invoice. If the seller doesn't collect the tax, the buyer owes it to the state as use tax.

How tax applies depends on your contract, so confirm with your accountant or the Comptroller of Maryland.
